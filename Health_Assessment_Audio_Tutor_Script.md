## PART 1 of 22. Welcome, and the nursing process

Welcome. This is your audio tutor for Health Assessment. Everything in your notes is here: history taking, physical assessment, vital signs, nursing assessment, and the respiratory, cardiovascular, abdominal and genitourinary examinations. We will not just read the slides. For each point you will hear what it is, why it is true, and what it changes at the bedside. Listen first, then read your notes. They will feel familiar.

One tip before we start. When you hear "Pause and answer", actually answer out loud. That is where the learning sticks.

Let us start with the frame that holds everything else. Nursing is the art of applying scientific principles in a humanitarian way. The nursing process is the organizing framework for that practice. It is a systematic, problem-solving method used to identify a patient's actual problems and the problems they are at risk of, and then to plan, deliver and evaluate care in an orderly, scientific way.

It has five phases. Assessment. Diagnosis. Planning. Implementation. Evaluation. It is drawn as a circle, not a line, because evaluation feeds straight back into assessment. You never finish assessing a patient.

Assessment is the first phase and the most critical one. Everything after it depends on it. If your data are wrong, your nursing diagnosis is wrong, your plan targets the wrong problem, and your evaluation measures the wrong thing. A beautiful care plan built on a missed finding is still a failed care plan.

Formally, assessment is the systematic and continuous collection, organization, validation and documentation of data. Four verbs, in that order: collect, organize, validate, document. Remember them. They are the steps of assessment, and exams love them.

What does assessment build? A database. The database is all the information about a client. It includes the nursing health history, the physical examination, the physician's history, and the results of laboratory and diagnostic tests.

Why do we assess? Your notes list eight purposes. To validate a diagnosis. To provide a basis for effective nursing care. To support effective decision making. To form the basis of an accurate diagnosis. To promote holistic care. To provide effective and innovative care. To collect data for nursing research. And to evaluate nursing care. Notice the pattern. Every purpose is about making a decision safer.

Now, the types of assessment. Your notes describe them in two places with slightly different names, so let us merge them cleanly. There are four types, plus one variant.

First, the initial comprehensive assessment, also called the admission assessment. It happens at first contact, within a set time after admission, in the hospital, clinic, community or home. Its purpose is to build a baseline. It covers the client's view of their own health, past history, family history, lifestyle and function, plus a full step-by-step physical examination. One of your slides calls this triage. Be careful with that word. Triage is really rapid sorting by urgency. The comprehensive assessment is the deep baseline that comes after.

Here is why the baseline matters. A blood pressure of 100 over 60 means nothing on its own. In a young woman whose usual reading is 100 over 60, it is normal. In a man whose baseline is 160 over 95, it could mean he is bleeding. Without a baseline, you cannot see change.

Second, the ongoing or partial assessment. This happens every time you meet the patient after the baseline exists. It is a mini overview. Problems already found are rechecked in less depth to see if they are better or worse. Normal systems get a brief look to catch new problems. Your notes give the example of a patient with lung cancer: frequent lung sounds, plus the colour and temperature of the extremities to judge oxygenation.

Third, the focused or problem-oriented assessment. This is a thorough look at one specific problem, and nothing unrelated. A patient with ear pain gets an ear, nose, mouth and throat assessment, not a full system review. In intensive care, a focused assessment may be repeated every few minutes. The nurse asks three questions. Does the problem still exist? Has it improved, worsened or resolved? Is there anything new, overlooked or misdiagnosed?

Fourth, the emergency assessment. A very rapid assessment in a life-threatening situation such as choking, cardiac arrest or drowning. The priority is preserving life, so you go straight to airway, breathing and circulation. It is not comprehensive, by design. Here is a point students miss. Emergencies are not only physical. A sudden suicidal statement, or social conflict heading toward violence, also triggers an emergency assessment.

The variant is the time-lapsed assessment. It compares current status with a baseline taken some time ago, such as clinic follow-ups, home visits, or child growth screenings. Think of it as the long-gap version of ongoing assessment.

Exam trap. If a question describes a patient who collapses and is not breathing, and asks which assessment you perform, it is the emergency assessment. Not comprehensive. Not focused. Life first, details later.

Pause and answer. A patient admitted yesterday with pneumonia is now reviewed on your morning round. You check his lung sounds, oxygen, temperature and breathing. Which type of assessment is that?

It is an ongoing, or partial, assessment. You are rechecking known problems against yesterday's baseline, and briefly scanning for new ones.

Recap. Assessment is the first and most critical phase of the nursing process. It means collecting, organizing, validating and documenting data. The four types are comprehensive, ongoing, focused and emergency, with time-lapsed as a variant.

## PART 2 of 22. Data: types, sources, methods and validation

Every nursing decision rests on data. In this part: what counts as data, where it comes from, how you collect it, and how you check it before you trust it.

Collecting data means gathering information about the whole person: physical, psychological, emotional, socio-cultural and spiritual factors. It includes past history, such as allergies, previous surgeries, chronic diseases and use of folk or traditional healing. It includes current problems, such as pain, nausea, sleep, religious practices, and the medications and treatments the patient is taking now.

Here is a real-world point on traditional medicine. Always ask about herbal or traditional remedies. Patients often do not mention them unless asked directly. Some affect the liver, the kidneys or bleeding, and some interact with prescribed drugs.

There are two types of data. Subjective data are symptoms, also called covert data. They are what the patient tells you. Nausea, the description of pain, fatigue. Only the patient can feel them.

Objective data are signs, also called overt data. They can be detected by an observer, measured, or tested against a standard. You can see, hear, feel or smell them. Skin discoloration. A temperature of 38.5 degrees Celsius. A wound that smells.

Memory hook. Symptoms are Subjective: both start with S, and the patient says them. Signs are seen.

Exam trap. "The patient states her pain is 8 out of 10." Subjective or objective? Subjective. The number does not make it objective. It is still the patient's report. But "the patient is grimacing and guarding her abdomen" is objective, because you observed it.

Sources of data. The primary source is the patient. In most cases the alert, oriented patient is the most accurate reporter of their own history and symptoms. Secondary sources are used when the patient cannot give information, because of confusion, age, or severity of illness. Secondary sources include family members, significant others, medical records, diagnostic results, and other health professionals such as doctors and other nurses.

Clinical reasoning here. When you use a secondary source, note it. "History given by the patient's daughter" changes how reliable the story is. The daughter may not know about the chest pain her father hid from everyone.

Three methods of data collection. Observing: gathering data with your senses. Interviewing: a planned conversation with a purpose. Examining: the physical examination, which is guided by what the patient told you. A head-to-toe approach is often used because it is systematic and stops you from skipping areas.

Now, the part that separates safe nurses from dangerous ones. Validation. Validation is double-checking data to confirm it is accurate and factual, because your nursing diagnoses and interventions will be built on it.

Validation has five purposes. To make sure data collection is complete. To make sure subjective and objective data agree. To find data you overlooked. To avoid jumping to conclusions. And to separate cues from inferences.

That last one matters. A cue is what you actually observed. An inference is your interpretation of it. "The patient is holding his abdomen" is a cue. "The patient has abdominal pain" is an inference. Your notes give exactly this example. The nurse assumed abdominal pain, but the patient was actually upset about his diagnosis. The inference was wrong. Validate before you act on it.

You do not revalidate everything. You would not normally repeat a temperature or blood pressure. But you must revalidate in three situations.

One. When subjective and objective data disagree. A man says he is very happy, despite just learning he has terminal cancer. That gap needs exploring.

Two. When the patient says different things at different times. She says she has never had surgery, then later mentions her appendix was removed years ago.

Three. When a finding is very abnormal and does not fit anything else. A temperature of 104 degrees Fahrenheit, that is 40 degrees Celsius, in a patient resting comfortably with skin that is warm but not flushed. Before you call the doctor, recheck it.

Methods of validation. Recheck your own data, for example with a different thermometer. Clarify with the patient by asking more questions. Verify with another professional, such as asking an experienced nurse to listen to the heart sound you think is abnormal. And compare your objective findings with the subjective ones. A woman says she never goes in the sun, but her skin is dark, wrinkled and sun-damaged. Explore that.

Bedside wisdom. The most dangerous number is a surprising number you did not recheck. Faulty equipment and wrong technique cause many "abnormal" readings. But the reverse is also true. Never repeat a reading endlessly just because you do not like it. If a recheck confirms it, act.

Pause and answer. A patient is holding his abdomen. You write "abdominal pain". What error have you made, and how do you fix it?

You recorded an inference as if it were a cue. You fix it by clarifying with the patient. Ask him what he is feeling, then record what you observed and what he said.

Recap. Subjective data are what the patient says, objective data are what you detect. The patient is the primary source, and everyone else is secondary. Validate when data conflict, change, or look extreme.

## PART 3 of 22. Organizing, documenting, and assessment tools

Data that sits in your head is useless to the next shift. In this part: how to organize data, how to document it properly, and the formal tools that measure function.

Organizing data means using a written or computerized format that arranges the assessment systematically, adapted to the patient's condition. Your notes give three frameworks.

First, the body systems model, also called the medical model or review of systems. It organizes data by major anatomical system. It is thorough for finding actual and potential problems in each organ.

Second, Gordon's functional health patterns. This focuses on how the person functions, not just their organs. It captures strengths as well as problems. There are eleven patterns. Health perception and health management. Nutritional and metabolic. Elimination. Activity and exercise. Sleep and rest. Cognitive and perceptual. Self-perception and self-concept. Role and relationship. Sexuality and reproduction. Coping and stress tolerance. Value and belief.

Why use Gordon? Because a nursing diagnosis is about the human response, not the disease. A man with a stroke has a medical diagnosis. Your nursing concerns are his mobility, his swallowing, his skin, his role as the family earner, and how he is coping.

Third, Maslow's hierarchy of needs, pictured in your notes. From the base up: physiological needs such as food, drink and oxygen. Safety and security. Love and belonging. Esteem. Aesthetic and cognitive needs. And self-actualization at the top. Its practical power is prioritizing. Lower needs usually come first. Airway beats anxiety about the hospital bill.

Now documentation. Documenting completes the assessment phase. It must be accurate and include all data collected. Here is the golden rule: record facts, not interpretations. Your notes give a perfect example. Do not write "appetite good." Write "coffee 240 millilitres, juice 120 millilitres, one egg." Anyone reading that can judge the appetite for themselves.

Why document? Your notes give eight reasons. It is a chronological record of the course of care. It is how the team communicates, preventing fragmentation, repetition and delays. It is a basis for screening and validating diagnoses. It helps identify new problems. It shows the educational needs of the patient and family. It supports eligibility for care and reimbursement. It is a permanent legal record of care that was, or was not, given. And it provides data for research and education.

The legal point deserves a sentence. In law, and in practice, if it was not documented, it is treated as not done.

Guidelines for documentation. Write legibly in ink that cannot be erased. Use correct grammar and spelling. Avoid wordiness, and use phrases rather than full sentences. Record the findings, not how you obtained them. Be objective, with no premature judgments or diagnoses. Record the patient's own understanding of their problem. Do not write the word "normal" for normal findings; describe what you found. Record symptoms in full detail. And support objective data with specific observations.

Why avoid "normal"? Because "normal" means different things to different nurses. "Lungs clear bilaterally, vesicular breath sounds, no added sounds" tells the next nurse exactly what the baseline was.

Now, what goes into a nursing assessment. Nursing assessment is holistic. Every patient needs a complete assessment of their needs, whatever the reason for the visit, usually guided by a nursing model. The output is a list of nursing problems, actual or potential. Your notes give the example of a patient immobile after a road traffic accident: potential for impaired skin integrity related to immobility.

The components. Biographic data: name, address, age, sex, marital status, occupation, religion. Reason for visit, or chief complaint. History of present illness. Past health history, including immunizations. Family history, which reveals risks such as diabetes, hypertension, cancer and mental illness. Review of systems. Lifestyle: habits, diet, sleep, daily activities, hobbies. Social data: family relationships, ethnic and educational background, economic status, home and neighbourhood. Psychological data: emotional state. And pattern of health care: which hospitals, health centres and providers they use.

Your notes add a psychological and social examination. It covers the patient's perception: why they think they are being assessed and what they hope to gain. Emotional health: mental state and coping style. Social health: housing, finances, relationships, family tree, employment, support networks. Physical health: sleep, appetite, weight, alcohol, tobacco, street drugs, and every medication with a comment on whether it works. Spiritual health: is religion important, and what gives them purpose? And intellectual health: cognition, hallucinations, delusions, concentration, interests.

Then the physical examination, which uses inspection, palpation, percussion and auscultation, plus vital signs and system examinations. The assessment is documented in the paper or electronic record, available to the whole team.

Finally, the assessment tools. Activities of daily living, or A-D-Ls, are the everyday tasks of self-care: feeding, bathing, dressing, grooming, plus work, homemaking and leisure.

The Barthel index measures independence in daily living and mobility. It has ten items. Feeding, 0, 5 or 10. Bathing, 0 or 5. Grooming, 0 or 5. Dressing, 0, 5 or 10. Bowels, 0, 5 or 10. Bladder, 0, 5 or 10. Toilet use, 0, 5 or 10. Transfers from bed to chair, 0 to 15. Mobility on level surfaces, 0 to 15. Stairs, 0, 5 or 10. The total runs from 0 to 100, and a higher score means more independent.

Memory hook. The two fifteen-point items are the two that move the whole body: transfers and walking. Bathing and grooming are the two small five-point items.

Bedside use. Barthel is not paperwork. A falling score tells you the patient is deteriorating or needs more help at home before discharge. A rising score during rehabilitation proves progress.

Your notes also name the General Health Questionnaire, a screening tool for psychological distress, and the Mental Status Examination. The Mental Status Examination is a structured set of questions and observations that gives a snapshot of the patient's current mental, cognitive and behavioural condition.

Pause and answer. Which is better documentation: "Patient ate well", or "Patient ate half a plate of rice and all of the beans"?

The second. It is factual and measurable. The first is an interpretation that no one else can check.

Recap. Organize data by body systems, Gordon's functional patterns, or Maslow for priorities. Document facts, not opinions, and avoid the word "normal". The Barthel index scores ten daily activities from 0 to 100.

## PART 4 of 22. History taking, part one: approach, profile, and the present illness

Here is a number that should change how you work. Around 70 percent of the time, the diagnosis can be made from the history alone. Not the scan. Not the lab. The conversation. In this part: what history taking is, how to approach the patient, and the first three components.

History taking is the process of gaining information by asking the patient specific questions, with the aim of forming a diagnosis and providing care. An accurate history is the critical first step in finding the cause, or aetiology, of an illness. Diagnosis rests on three pillars, in this order: clinical history, physical examination, investigations. The history comes first because it tells you where to look.

Sir William Osler put it best: always listen to the patient; they might be telling you the diagnosis.

The foundation of a true history is good communication. It takes practice, patience, understanding and concentration.

The approach. Your appearance matters, so dress professionally. Introduce yourself and build rapport. Be alert and give full attention. Make sure consent has been given. Maintain privacy and dignity. Make the patient as comfortable as possible. Summarize each stage as you go. And involve the patient in the process.

One line from your notes is worth memorizing. If you are in a bad mood or distracted, you can end up making a history rather than taking one. In other words, a distracted clinician fills the gaps with assumptions.

There are seven components. Patient's profile. Chief complaint. History of present illness. Past medical history. Family history. Socioeconomic history. System review.

Component one: the patient's profile. Date and time. Name. Age. Sex. Religion. Marital status. Occupation. Address. And who gave the history.

Each item earns its place. Age narrows the likely diseases. Occupation reveals exposures: a miner's dust, a farmer's pesticides. Religion may affect decisions such as blood transfusion. Address tells you about endemic infections, travel time to care, and follow-up. And "who gave the history" tells you how reliable it is.

Component two: the chief complaint. This is the main reason the patient is seeking help. It is usually one symptom, sometimes more, such as fever, headache or pain. It is recorded in the patient's own words, with its duration.

How to ask. "What brings you here?" "How can I help you?" "What seems to be the problem?" If there are several complaints, list them in chronological order. Your notes give an example: fever for 2 weeks, productive cough for 1 week, vomiting for 2 days, fatigue for 1 day.

Exam trap. The chief complaint is recorded in the patient's words, not in diagnostic terms. "Chest pain for 3 hours" is correct. "Myocardial infarction" is not a complaint; it is a diagnosis.

Component three: the history of present illness. This is the heart of the history. You expand the chief complaint in detail and ask about associated symptoms. Always start with an open question and let the patient tell their story. Once they finish, use closed questions to clarify. Avoid leading questions.

Three question types. Open questions let patients express their own thoughts: "Is there anything else you want to mention?" Closed questions ask for facts: "When did this pain start?" Leading questions carry your assumption and push the patient toward the answer you expect: "The pain goes to your left arm, doesn't it?"

Why avoid leading questions? Because anxious patients often agree with the clinician. You end up confirming your own guess instead of discovering the truth.

What to cover: time of onset, how it evolved, any tests, treatment and outcome, and associated positive and negative symptoms. Use plain language, not medical terms. Present it in sequence, relative to the day of admission, in full detail.

Why do negative symptoms matter? A patient with a cough but no fever, no weight loss and no night sweats is a very different patient from one who has all three. Negatives rule diagnoses out.

For any symptom, especially pain, use SOCRATES. Site: where is it? Onset: when and how did it start, sudden or gradual? Character: sharp, dull, burning, crushing? Radiation: does it spread? Associated symptoms: what else comes with it? Timing: constant or on and off, and how has it changed? Exacerbating and relieving factors: what makes it worse or better? Severity: how bad, often scored 0 to 10.

Your slides list the letters with alleviating and exacerbating as separate items, which is the same content. Use whichever form your lecturer uses in the exam.

Clinical reasoning with SOCRATES. Crushing central chest pain that spreads to the left arm or jaw, with sweating, suggests the heart. Sharp chest pain that is worse on breathing in suggests the pleura. The same organ area can hide different diseases. The character and radiation separate them.

Your notes include a model history of present illness. A man was well until one week before admission, when he fell while gardening and cut his foot on a stone. That evening the foot swelled and he could not walk. The next day a private clinic gave him oral medicines he cannot name. There was no improvement. Two days before admission the wound began discharging pus, with high fever, rigors, nausea and vomiting.

Notice what makes it good. It is chronological. It is anchored to admission. It includes treatment and outcome. And it tells a story that already points to a diagnosis: a wound infection spreading into the blood, with signs of sepsis. As a nurse, that story makes you check his vital signs, his blood glucose and his foot pulses urgently.

Pause and answer. A patient says "I have chest pain." You ask, "Does it spread to your left arm?" What kind of question is that, and what should you ask instead?

It is a leading question. Ask an open one instead: "Does the pain move anywhere?" Let the patient tell you.

Recap. History gives about 70 percent of diagnoses. Record the chief complaint in the patient's own words, with its duration. Open questions first, closed questions to clarify, and avoid leading ones. Use SOCRATES for every symptom.

## PART 5 of 22. History taking, part two: past, family, social, and system review

The present illness tells you what is happening now. The rest of the history tells you why it is happening to this person. In this part: the last four components, and how to close the interview.

Component four: past medical history. Ask about any similar complaint in the past. Other medical problems, past or present. Chronic diseases such as hypertension and diabetes. Past hospitalizations and surgeries. Past medications, with dose and duration. Allergies. For children: birth history, developmental milestones and immunizations. For women: gynaecological and obstetric history.

Two bedside points. First, allergies. Always ask what happened, not just what they are allergic to. "Penicillin gave me a mild rash" and "Penicillin made my throat close" are very different allergies. Second, chronic diseases change how you read every finding. Diabetes hides infection and delays wound healing. Hypertension changes what a "normal" blood pressure is for that patient.

Component five: family history. You are looking for genetically transmitted diseases and conditions that run in families. Ask whether any illness runs in the family, whether relatives have had similar complaints, whether parents or siblings have chronic illnesses, and, if parents have died, at what age and from what.

Tailor it to the complaint. Your notes give a strong example. A patient with anaemia: ask about sickle cell disease, thalassaemia and G6PD deficiency, all of which are inherited. G6PD deficiency matters practically, because some drugs, including certain antimalarials, can trigger haemolysis in these patients.

Component six: socioeconomic history. Smoking: amount, duration and type. Alcohol: amount, duration and type. Any drug use. Sexual history, if a sexually transmitted infection is suspected. Occupation, social and educational background, and finances.

For smoking, calculate pack-years. Packs per day, multiplied by years smoked. One pack a day for twenty years is twenty pack-years. You will need this number again in the respiratory module.

Why ask about finances? Because a perfect treatment plan fails if the patient cannot afford the drugs, the transport to follow-up, or the special diet. Your plan must be one the patient can actually follow.

Component seven: the system review. This is a head-to-toe checklist of symptoms, used to catch anything the patient forgot or did not think was related.

General: weakness, fatigue, loss of appetite, weight change, fever, lumps, night sweats.

Cardiovascular: chest pain. Paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea, which is waking at night suddenly breathless. Orthopnoea, breathlessness when lying flat. Shortness of breath. Cough and sputum. Palpitations. Cyanosis.

Clinical reasoning. Orthopnoea and paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea both point strongly toward heart failure. Lying flat returns more blood to a failing heart, and the lungs congest. Ask how many pillows they sleep on. A patient who has gone from one pillow to four is getting worse.

Respiratory: cough, dry or productive. Sputum colour, amount and smell. Haemoptysis, coughing blood. Chest pain. Shortness of breath. Fast breathing. Hoarseness. Wheezing.

Gastrointestinal: appetite, diet, nausea and vomiting, regurgitation, heartburn and flatulence, difficulty swallowing, abdominal pain or distension, change in bowel habit, jaundice. Haematemesis, vomiting blood. Melaena, black tarry stool, which means bleeding high in the gut, digested on its way down.

Urinary: frequency, dysuria, urgency, hesitancy, terminal dribbling, nocturia, back or loin pain, incontinence, the character, amount and timing of urine, and fever.

Genital: pain, discomfort or itching, discharge, unusual bleeding.

Nervous system: vision, smell, taste, hearing and speech. Headache. Fits, faints, blackouts or loss of consciousness. Muscle weakness, numbness or paralysis. Abnormal sensation. Change in behaviour.

Musculoskeletal: pain in muscle, bone or joint. Swelling. Weakness or reduced movement. Deformities.

The nursing assessment notes expand the review of systems further. General: fever, chills, malaise, pain, sleep, fatigue. Diet: appetite, likes and dislikes, restrictions. Skin, hair and nails: rash, itching, colour change, sweating, abnormal growth. Eyes: acuity, blurring, double vision, photophobia. Ears: hearing loss, pain, discharge, tinnitus, vertigo. Nose: smell, frequent colds, obstruction, nosebleeds, sinus pain. Throat and mouth: hoarseness, sore throat, bleeding gums, tooth problems. Endocrine: thyroid swelling, heat or cold intolerance, unexplained weight change, excessive urination and thirst. For men: puberty, erections, testicular pain, libido, fertility. For women: menstrual history, last menstrual period, pregnancies, miscarriages, deliveries, contraception, breast symptoms. Chest: exposure to tuberculosis and the last chest X-ray. Psychiatric: depression, mood change, poor concentration, nervousness, irritability, and suicidal thoughts. For children, add growth measurements and a neuromuscular assessment.

The complete health history, as listed in your nursing assessment notes, is: biographical data, reason for seeking care, present illness, past health, accidents and injuries, hospitalizations and operations, family history, review of systems, functional assessment of daily living, and the patient's own perception of their health.

Closing the history. Summarize what you heard. Ask whether you understood correctly. Ask if there is anything else they want you to know. Explain your plan. And check they agree with it.

That final question, "Is there anything else?", is often where the real worry comes out. Many patients mention their most important concern last.

Pause and answer. A patient with anaemia gives you his history. Which three inherited conditions do your notes tell you to rule out in the family history?

Sickle cell disease, thalassaemia, and G6PD deficiency.

Recap. Past history covers old illnesses, surgery, drugs and allergies. Family history is tailored to the complaint. Social history includes pack-years and whether the patient can afford the plan. The system review catches what the patient forgot. Close by summarizing and agreeing a plan.

## PART 6 of 22. Physical assessment: setting, tools, inspection and palpation

Your hands and eyes are diagnostic instruments. In this part: what physical assessment is, how to prepare, the order of techniques, and the first two, inspection and palpation.

Physical assessment is the systematic, ongoing collection of patient data using inspection, palpation, percussion and auscultation. Its purpose is to gather physical and mental information about the patient. It is the groundwork for every nursing intervention. Being able to tell normal from abnormal makes the nurse more confident and improves patient experience and outcomes.

Your physical assessment notes list the same four types we covered in part one: comprehensive, ongoing, focused and emergency. Same definitions, same examples.

To be good at it, you need three things. You must be able to use your tools, prepare the setting, and perform the four techniques.

The tools include the penlight, the sphygmomanometer, the otoscope for ears, the ophthalmoscope for eyes, tuning forks, and the stethoscope. Add a watch with a second hand, a thermometer, a tape measure and gloves.

Preparing the setting. The room should be warm and comfortable, because cold makes patients shiver and tense their muscles, which ruins palpation. It should be private: close the door, draw the curtain. Quiet: turn off the radio and television. Well lit, ideally with daylight. The bed or table should be firm and at a height that stops you stooping. And keep a tray or table for your equipment.

The order of techniques is inspection, palpation, percussion, auscultation. Why this order? Because touching can change what you see, and pressing and tapping can change what you hear.

One exception, and it is a classic exam question. In the abdomen, the order is inspection, auscultation, percussion, palpation. You listen before you touch, because percussion and palpation stimulate the bowel and change the bowel sounds.

Memory hook for the abdomen: "I Always Prefer Peace." Inspect, Auscultate, Percuss, Palpate.

Technique one: inspection. Inspection uses sight, smell and hearing to detect normal and abnormal findings. It starts the moment you meet the patient and continues throughout the examination. Sometimes it needs equipment, such as the ophthalmoscope or otoscope.

Guidelines. Look before you touch. Fully expose the part you are inspecting, and drape the rest. Note colour, pattern, size, location, consistency, symmetry, movement, behaviour and odours. Compare both sides of the body. And avoid fluorescent lighting, because it distorts skin colour. That matters when you are looking for jaundice or cyanosis.

The general survey is your first inspection of the whole person. Appearance: apparent age, skin colour, facial features. Body structure: height, nutrition, posture, position and symmetry. Mobility: gait and range of movement. Behaviour: facial expression, mood, speech, dress and hygiene. Cognition: level of consciousness and orientation to person, place, time and situation, often written as "oriented times four". And any sign of distress, such as grimacing or laboured breathing.

Real-life insight. The general survey often tells you who is sick before any number does. A patient sitting bolt upright, leaning forward, unable to finish a sentence, is in respiratory distress, whatever the monitor says.

Technique two: palpation. Palpation uses parts of the hand to feel for texture, rough or smooth. Temperature, warm or cold. Moisture, dry or wet. Mobility, fixed, movable, or vibrating. Consistency, soft, hard or fluid-filled. Pulse strength, strong, weak, thready or bounding. Size. Shape, well defined or irregular. And degree of tenderness.

Different parts of the hand do different jobs. The finger pads are used most. They detect fine detail: texture, moisture, masses, pulsations, oedema, crepitus, and the size, shape, position, mobility and consistency of organs.

The palmar surface at the knuckle joints, the ball of the hand, and the ulnar edge of the hand best detect vibration, such as a cardiac thrill or vocal fremitus.

The back of the hand is most sensitive to temperature. That is why you check a forehead or a limb's warmth with the back of your hand, not the palm.

Memory hook. Pads for detail, palm and ulnar edge for vibration, back of hand for temperature.

There are three types of palpation. Light palpation always comes first. It is gentle and superficial, pressing less than 1 centimetre. Use it for pulses, tenderness, skin texture, temperature and moisture.

Deep palpation explores internal structures, to a depth of about 4 to 5 centimetres or more. It reveals the position, size, shape, mobility and consistency of organs and masses, and areas of discomfort. It is used mainly in abdominal and genital examination. Palpable organs can include the liver, an enlarged kidney, the gallbladder, the spleen, the aorta, the uterus and the ovaries.

Note a small inconsistency. Your abdominal notes say deep palpation presses 5 to 8 centimetres. Both appear in standard textbooks. Know that deep palpation goes several centimetres deeper than light palpation, and quote the figure your lecturer uses.

Bimanual palpation uses two hands, one on each side of the part. One hand presses, the other feels. It is used for the uterus, breasts and spleen.

Bedside rule. Always ask about pain first, and palpate the painful area last. If you start on the tender spot, the patient guards, their muscles tighten, and the rest of the examination becomes useless.

Pause and answer. You want to know if a patient's leg is cooler than the other. Which part of your hand do you use?

The back of your hand, because it is most sensitive to temperature.

Recap. The order is inspection, palpation, percussion, auscultation, except in the abdomen, where auscultation comes second. Inspect before touching, and compare both sides. Pads for detail, palm for vibration, back of hand for temperature. Light palpation before deep, and the painful area last.

## PART 7 of 22. Percussion and auscultation

In this part you learn to hear density: how tapping reveals whether an area holds air, fluid or solid tissue, and how to use the stethoscope properly.

Percussion is tapping a body surface with fingers, hands or small instruments to judge the size, consistency and borders of organs, and whether they contain air or fluid. The chest and abdomen are the areas percussed most often. The key principle: the denser the tissue underneath, the duller and quieter the sound.

There are five percussion sounds. Learn them from least dense to most dense.

Hyperresonance. Very loud, very low-pitched, long, booming. Heard over over-inflated lung, as in emphysema, and in pneumothorax. Not normal in adults.

Tympany. Loud, high-pitched, drum-like. Heard over air in a hollow organ, like the gastric air bubble or a puffed-out cheek. Your notes also list it over a large pneumothorax.

Resonance. Loud, low-pitched, long, hollow. The normal sound over healthy lung, which is mostly air mixed with some tissue.

Dullness. Medium intensity, medium pitch, thud-like. Heard over dense organs, such as the liver, heart, a full bladder or an enlarged spleen. When it appears over the lung, it means fluid or solid has replaced air, as in pneumonia, collapse, or pleural effusion.

Flatness. Soft, high-pitched, short. Heard over very dense tissue, such as the thigh muscle, bone, or a tumour.

A note on your slides. One slide says tympany is heard when the chest contains free air in pneumothorax. Other tables say hyperresonance. Both appear in textbooks, because a large pneumothorax can sound tympanic. For exams, the most common teaching is: pneumothorax is hyperresonant.

Clinical reasoning. Percussion tells you what replaced the air. If one lung base is dull and the other resonant, something dense is sitting at that base: fluid, like an effusion, or consolidated lung, like pneumonia. Your hands have just localized a problem without an X-ray.

There are three types of percussion. Direct, or immediate, percussion taps the body directly with one or two fingertips, often to find tenderness, for example over the sinuses. Indirect, or mediate, percussion is the most common. You place the middle finger of one hand flat on the skin and strike it with the middle finger of the other hand. The sound varies with the density underneath. And blunt percussion: you place one hand flat on the body and strike it with the fist of the other hand. It detects tenderness over organs such as the kidneys.

Technique tip for indirect percussion. Press only the middle finger firmly on the skin, and lift the rest of your hand off. Strike with a quick wrist movement, not the whole arm, and lift the striking finger straight off, like a piano hammer. A slow strike muffles the sound.

Now auscultation. Auscultation is listening to body sounds, usually with a stethoscope. It is used for the heart, blood vessels, lungs, abdomen, and the fetal heart in pregnancy.

The stethoscope has two chest pieces. The diaphragm is flat and best for high-pitched sounds: normal breath sounds, bowel sounds and normal heart sounds. Press it firmly. The bell is cup-shaped and best for soft, low-pitched sounds: some abnormal heart sounds, extra heart sounds, and bruits. A bruit is an abnormal blowing or whooshing sound over a vessel, caused by turbulent blood flow. Hold the bell lightly. If you press the bell hard, the skin stretches across it and turns it into a diaphragm, and the low sounds vanish.

Memory hook. Diaphragm: high and firm. Bell: low and light.

Guidelines for auscultation. Remove distracting noise, such as radio, television and machines. Expose the area and never listen through clothes or gowns, because rubbing fabric creates false sounds. Use the diaphragm for high-pitched sounds and the bell for low-pitched sounds.

Two more real-world tips. Warm the chest piece in your hand first. A cold diaphragm makes patients flinch and breathe unnaturally. And watch out for body hair, which crackles under the diaphragm and can be mistaken for lung crackles. Pressing firmly helps.

Pause and answer. You percuss the lower right chest of a patient with fever and cough, and it is dull. The left side is resonant. What does the dullness tell you?

Something denser than air has filled that area, such as consolidation from pneumonia or a pleural effusion.

Recap. Percussion sounds, from least to most dense: hyperresonance, tympany, resonance, dullness, flatness. Indirect percussion is the most common type, and blunt percussion checks organ tenderness. Diaphragm for high-pitched sounds, pressed firmly. Bell for low-pitched sounds, held lightly.

## PART 8 of 22. Vital signs: temperature

The vital, or cardinal, signs are body temperature, pulse, respiration and blood pressure. They reflect the state of the cardiovascular, peripheral vascular and respiratory systems, among others. Nurses usually begin the hands-on examination with vital signs. In practice, most wards also record oxygen saturation, and pain is often called the fifth vital sign.

Why do vital signs matter so much? Because deterioration usually shows in the vital signs hours before a patient collapses. A rising breathing rate is often the earliest warning of all.

Body temperature is the balance between heat produced and heat lost. It is measured in degrees.

There are two kinds. Core temperature is the temperature of deep tissues, such as the abdominal and pelvic cavities. It stays relatively constant, around 37 degrees Celsius. Surface temperature is the temperature of the skin, subcutaneous tissue and fat. It rises and falls with the environment. The average body temperature ranges from 36.1 to 37.2 degrees Celsius, which is 97 to 99 degrees Fahrenheit.

Factors that influence body temperature. Hormones: thyroid hormone raises it. Progesterone, released after ovulation, raises it until menstruation. Environment: seasons, and hot or cold baths, cause temporary changes. Exercise raises it. Eating raises it slightly, through the energy cost of digesting food. Stress raises it, because adrenaline and noradrenaline increase metabolism and heat production.

Age matters too. Children have less mature temperature control. Normal temperature falls with age: about 37.2 degrees Celsius in young children, 37 in adults, and about 36 in older adults.

Clinical reasoning on older adults. Because their baseline is lower and their fever response is weaker, an older adult with serious infection may only reach 37.5 degrees, or may even be cold. New confusion in an older person can be the first sign of infection. Do not wait for a high fever.

Temperature sites. Oral is the most convenient site for an alert adult, with a normal of about 37 degrees Celsius. Wait 15 minutes if the patient has just smoked, or had hot or iced drinks, because these falsely change the reading.

Rectal. Your notes say it is preferred for confused or comatose patients, those who cannot close their mouth, and those receiving oxygen. Normal is about 37.5 degrees Celsius, slightly higher than oral. Know the safety side too. Rectal temperature is usually avoided in patients with rectal surgery or injury, very low white cell counts, or a bleeding risk, and it can stimulate the vagus nerve and slow the heart in cardiac patients.

Axillary is safe and used for infants and small children. Normal is about 36.5 degrees Celsius. It is safe but less accurate than core sites, so a borderline reading in a sick child should be confirmed.

Tympanic uses an infrared sensor to detect heat from the eardrum. It is fast and comfortable, but needs correct technique: in adults, pull the ear up and back to straighten the canal.

Memory hook for normal values. Rectal is highest, oral is middle, axillary is lowest. Roughly: 37.5, 37, 36.5. Half a degree apart.

Temperature can be recorded in Celsius or Fahrenheit. To convert Celsius to Fahrenheit, multiply by 1.8 and add 32. To convert Fahrenheit to Celsius, subtract 32 and divide by 1.8.

Quick example. 38 degrees Celsius: 38 times 1.8 is 68.4, plus 32 gives 100.4 degrees Fahrenheit. That number, 100.4, is the classic fever threshold you will see in many textbooks.

Pause and answer. A 78-year-old woman is newly confused. Her temperature is 37.4 degrees Celsius. Can you rule out infection?

No. Older adults have a lower baseline and a weaker fever response. New confusion can itself be the sign of infection, so assess further and report.

Recap. Normal core temperature is about 37 degrees Celsius. Rectal is the highest reading, then oral, then axillary. Hormones, exercise, food, stress and age all shift temperature. In older adults, infection may cause little or no fever.

## PART 9 of 22. Vital signs: pulse

The pulse is the rhythmic expansion of an artery caused by each heartbeat. It reflects the number of heartbeats per minute. Its character tells you about cardiac function and how well the peripheral tissues are perfused. You assess three things: rate, rhythm and volume.

Normal adult rate. One slide says 50 to 100 beats per minute. Another says bradycardia is 60 or less, and the age table gives 60 to 100 for adults. The standard teaching, and the one to use, is 60 to 100 beats per minute. Above 100 is tachycardia. Below 60 is bradycardia.

A peripheral pulse is felt away from the heart, such as at the wrist, foot or neck. The apical pulse is a central pulse, heard with a stethoscope at the apex of the heart, in the fifth intercostal space at the midclavicular line on the left.

Pulse sites from head to toe. Temporal, in front of the ear. Carotid, in the neck. Apical, at the heart. Brachial, on the inner elbow. Radial, at the thumb side of the wrist. Ulnar, at the little-finger side of the wrist. Femoral, in the groin. Popliteal, behind the knee. Posterior tibial, behind the inner ankle bone. Dorsalis pedis, on top of the foot.

Which site when? Radial for routine checks. Carotid in cardiac arrest, because it persists when peripheral pulses are lost. Brachial in infants. Apical for infants, irregular rhythms, and before giving drugs like digoxin.

Factors affecting the pulse. Age: the rate falls from birth to adulthood. Your notes give averages with ranges. Newborn about 130, range 80 to 180. One year, 120. Five to eight years, 100. Ten years, 70. Teenager, 75. Adult, 80, range 60 to 100. Older adult, 70.

Gender: after puberty, men's pulses are slightly lower than women's. Exercise increases the rate. Fever increases it, through vasodilation lowering blood pressure and through higher metabolism. Medications: digoxin slows it, adrenaline speeds it up. Hypovolaemia, loss of blood volume, increases it. Stress increases both rate and force. Position: standing up pools blood in the legs, reducing venous return, so blood pressure dips briefly and the heart rate rises. And disease, especially heart disease or anything impairing oxygenation.

Clinical reasoning. Tachycardia is often the body's first compensation for blood loss or dehydration. The heart speeds up to protect blood pressure. So a rising pulse with a normal blood pressure is not reassuring. It can mean the patient is compensating now and will drop their pressure later.

Rhythm is the pattern of beats and the intervals between them. In a normal pulse, the time between beats is equal. An irregular rhythm is called a dysrhythmia or arrhythmia. If the pulse is irregular, count for a full 60 seconds, not 30 seconds doubled.

Volume, also called strength or amplitude, is the force of blood with each beat. A bounding pulse is full and hard to press closed. A normal pulse is easily felt, does not fade in and out, and is not easily blocked. A weak, feeble or thready pulse reflects low volume, as in shock or dehydration.

Linking forward. A bounding pulse can also be a sign of carbon dioxide retention in respiratory failure, which we will meet in the respiratory module.

Pause and answer. An adult patient after surgery has a pulse of 118, and a blood pressure of 118 over 76, the same as before surgery. Is she stable?

Not necessarily. Tachycardia can be early compensation for bleeding or dehydration, before the blood pressure falls. Look at the wound and drains, her urine output and skin, and report.

Recap. The normal adult pulse is 60 to 100 beats per minute. Check rate, rhythm and volume. Count irregular pulses for a full minute. A rising pulse can warn of blood loss before the blood pressure drops.

## PART 10 of 22. Vital signs: respiration and breathing patterns

Respiration is the vital sign most often skipped, estimated, or recorded without being counted. It is also the earliest warning of deterioration. Count it properly, for a full minute, ideally without the patient knowing, because people change their breathing when they know they are being watched. A good trick is to keep your fingers on the pulse after counting it, and quietly watch the chest.

The respiratory rate is the number of breaths per minute. You assess rate, depth and pattern.

The normal adult rate. Your notes give 12 to 20 in one place, 12 to 16 in another, and 14 to 18 in the respiratory deck. Use 12 to 20 breaths per minute as your standard adult range. By age: newborns 30 to 60, early childhood 20 to 40, late childhood 15 to 25.

Tachypnoea is a rate above 20 per minute. One of your slides mistakenly labels this "tachycardia". The correct term is tachypnoea. Physiological causes include exercise, emotion and pregnancy. Pathological causes include pain, pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, asthma, foreign body aspiration, anxiety, and sepsis.

Bradypnoea is a rate below 12 per minute. It happens when a respiratory condition worsens toward respiratory failure, or with central nervous system depressants such as alcohol, opioids and benzodiazepines.

Red flag. A patient on opioids for pain who becomes drowsy and whose breathing slows is heading toward respiratory arrest. Wake them, assess, and report urgently. Sleepiness comes before the breathing slows, so treat sleepiness as a warning.

Apnoea is complete cessation of airflow to the lungs. Your notes define it as 15 seconds or more. It occurs in cardiopulmonary arrest, airway obstruction, and overdose of opioids or benzodiazepines.

Now depth. Hyperventilation is rapid, deep breathing that over-expands the lungs. Causes include anxiety, strenuous exercise and diabetic ketoacidosis. Hypoventilation is reduced rate and depth, for example from excessive sedation.

Clinical reasoning. Hyperventilation blows off carbon dioxide. That is why anxious hyperventilating patients get tingling fingers and lips. Hypoventilation does the reverse: carbon dioxide builds up and the blood becomes acidic.

Now the named patterns. These are high yield, and your slides mix up two of them, so learn these clean definitions.

Kussmaul breathing. Deep, laboured breathing, usually fast and regular. It is the body trying to blow off acid. It is classic in diabetic ketoacidosis and renal failure, both causes of metabolic acidosis. Memory hook: Kussmaul equals Ketoacidosis.

Cheyne-Stokes breathing. Breaths that gradually get deeper, then gradually shallower, then a period of apnoea, and then the cycle repeats. Think of a wave rising and falling, then a pause. It is seen in heart failure, raised intracranial pressure, brain injury, and drug overdose, and it is common near the end of life.

Biot's breathing. Irregular clusters of quick, shallow breaths of roughly equal depth, broken by irregular periods of apnoea. There is no smooth wave. It suggests damage to the lower brainstem, from raised intracranial pressure, space-occupying lesions, or meningitis.

Memory hook. Cheyne-Stokes is Crescendo then silence. Biot's is Broken and chaotic.

Your picture slide adds a few more. Sighing: frequent deeper breaths. Air trapping: increasing difficulty getting the breath out, as in asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. And ataxic breathing: complete disorganization with irregular depth, another sign of severe brainstem damage.

Factors that increase the respiratory rate: exercise, stress, a hot environment, and low oxygen at high altitude. Factors that decrease it: a cold environment, raised intracranial pressure, and drugs such as opioids.

Pause and answer. A young patient with type 1 diabetes has deep, rapid, sighing breaths and a fruity smell on his breath. Name the breathing pattern and the likely cause.

Kussmaul breathing, from diabetic ketoacidosis. The fruity smell is ketones.

Recap. The normal adult respiratory rate is 12 to 20. Count it for a full minute without the patient noticing. Kussmaul is deep breathing in ketoacidosis. Cheyne-Stokes rises and falls, then pauses. Biot's is chaotic clusters with apnoea, from brainstem damage.

## PART 11 of 22. Vital signs: blood pressure

Blood pressure is the pressure exerted by blood on the walls of the arteries. It varies with the cardiac cycle: highest during systole, lowest during diastole.

Systolic pressure is the pressure when the ventricles contract. Diastolic pressure is the pressure when the ventricles are at rest. Diastolic pressure is the lower pressure, present at all times in the arteries.

Pulse pressure is the difference between the systolic and diastolic pressures. It reflects stroke volume, the amount of blood ejected with each beat. For a blood pressure of 120 over 80, the pulse pressure is 40.

Clinical reasoning on pulse pressure. A narrowing pulse pressure, for example 100 over 85, can mean the stroke volume is falling, as in bleeding or cardiac tamponade. A widening pulse pressure is seen in aortic regurgitation, and in raised intracranial pressure as part of Cushing's triad, together with a slow heart rate and irregular breathing.

Blood pressure is measured in millimetres of mercury and written as a fraction, systolic over diastolic. The average healthy adult reading is 120 over 80.

The equipment. The sphygmomanometer, either an aneroid dial or a digital device, and a stethoscope for manual readings. The usual site is the upper arm, using the brachial artery. The thigh, using the popliteal artery, is used when the arms cannot be used.

Classification, based on the average of two or more readings in the clinic. This is the American Heart Association and American College of Cardiology system your notes use.

Normal: systolic below 120 and diastolic below 80.

Elevated: systolic 120 to 129, with diastolic still below 80.

Stage 1 hypertension: systolic 130 to 139, or diastolic 80 to 89.

Stage 2 hypertension: systolic 140 or more, or diastolic 90 or more.

Exam trap. Notice the word "or". If either number reaches a stage, the patient is in that stage. A reading of 128 over 88 is not "elevated". The diastolic of 88 puts it in stage 1.

Factors affecting blood pressure. Race: your notes state that African American men over 35 have higher blood pressure than European American men of the same age. Gender: after puberty, women usually have lower pressure than men of the same age, and after menopause their pressure generally rises. Medications can raise or lower it. Obesity in children and adults predisposes to hypertension. Diurnal variation: pressure is lowest early in the morning and peaks in the late afternoon or early evening. And any disease that affects cardiac output, blood volume, blood viscosity, or the stiffness of the arteries.

Now the most practical slide in the deck: common errors. Your notes list eight. Learn which direction each one pushes the reading, because that is what exams and real life test.

Cuff too narrow: falsely high.

Cuff too wide: falsely low.

Memory hook: a narrow cuff squeezes, so the pressure reads high.

Arm unsupported: falsely high, because the arm muscles are working.

Arm above the level of the heart: falsely low. And, the other way round, an arm below heart level reads falsely high.

Not enough rest before measuring: falsely high. The patient should rest for at least five minutes first.

Repeating the measurement too quickly: inaccurate, usually falsely high, because the veins are still congested. Wait one to two minutes between readings.

Deflating too quickly: you miss the first sound, so the systolic reads falsely low, and the diastolic may read falsely high.

Deflating too slowly: venous congestion falsely raises the diastolic, and it is painful for the patient.

The right cuff size. The bladder of the cuff should wrap around about 80 percent of the arm's circumference, and its width should be about 40 percent of the circumference. In practice: use the size markers on the cuff, and do not force a small cuff on a large arm.

Real-life link. In a busy clinic, most "hypertension" found on a single rushed reading from an unsupported arm, with a small cuff, after the patient climbed stairs, is measurement error. That is why the classification requires an average of two or more readings on separate occasions.

Pause and answer. You use a standard adult cuff on a very muscular, large arm. Will the reading be falsely high or falsely low?

Falsely high. The cuff is too narrow for that arm.

Recap. Systolic is ventricular contraction, diastolic is rest, and pulse pressure is the difference between them. Normal is below 120 over 80. Stage 2 hypertension is 140 or more, or 90 or more. A narrow cuff, an unsupported arm, or no rest reads high. A wide cuff or an arm above the heart reads low.

## PART 12 of 22. Respiratory system: how breathing works

You cannot read a chest examination without knowing what the lungs are doing underneath. In this part: the structure of the airway, the pleura, the mechanics of breathing, lung volumes, the control of breathing, and gas exchange. Keep one goal in mind. Every fact here explains a finding you will meet at the bedside.

The respiratory tract runs from the nose to the alveoli, and includes the blood supply. It is divided into two parts. Your notes place the upper tract as the nose, pharynx, larynx and trachea. The lower tract is the bronchi, bronchioles, alveolar ducts and alveoli. Be aware that many textbooks draw the line at the larynx and put the trachea in the lower tract, so use your lecturer's version.

A more useful division is by function. The conducting portion only moves air: the nose, nasal cavity, pharynx, larynx, trachea, and airways from the main bronchi down to the terminal bronchioles. The respiratory portion carries out gas exchange: respiratory bronchioles, alveolar ducts and alveoli.

The right lung has three lobes: upper, middle and lower. The left has two: upper and lower. The left is smaller to make room for the heart. The chest wall, meaning the ribs, pleura and respiratory muscles, is essential to breathing too.

Clinical link. The right main bronchus is wider, shorter and more vertical than the left. That is why inhaled foreign bodies and aspirated vomit usually go to the right lung.

The pleura. Two layers of membrane with a thin film of intrapleural fluid between them. The fluid has two jobs. It lubricates, preventing friction between the layers. And it helps create the negative intrapleural pressure that keeps the lungs expanded against the chest wall.

In disease, the pleural space fills with something it should not hold. Air gives a pneumothorax. Watery fluid gives a hydrothorax, or pleural effusion. Blood gives a haemothorax. Pus gives a pyothorax, also called an empyema.

Respiration as a whole has five stages. Ventilation. Gas exchange between the lungs and blood. Gas transport in the blood. Gas exchange between the blood and body cells. And cellular respiration. External respiration is exchange between lungs and blood. Internal respiration is exchange between blood and tissues.

Breathing has two phases. In normal quiet breathing, inspiration is active and expiration is passive.

Inspiration. Atmospheric pressure is the force that pushes air in. The diaphragm contracts and moves down, and the external intercostal muscles lift the ribs. The chest cavity gets bigger. Because of surface tension between the two pleural layers, the lungs follow the chest wall and expand. The pressure inside the lungs falls below atmospheric pressure, and air flows in. Surfactant stops the alveoli sticking together and collapsing when the pressure inside them is low.

Clinical link. Premature babies lack surfactant. Their alveoli collapse with every breath, causing respiratory distress syndrome.

Expiration. It comes from the elastic recoil of lung and muscle tissue, and the surface tension in the alveoli. Forced expiration adds the abdominal and chest wall muscles, which push the abdomen up against the diaphragm.

Clinical link. In emphysema, elastic recoil is destroyed. Expiration stops being passive. Patients work to breathe out, often through pursed lips, and air gets trapped.

Lung volumes are measured by spirometry. Here are the four volumes, with your notes' values.

Tidal volume: the air moved in one normal breath, about 500 millilitres.

Inspiratory reserve volume: the extra air you can force in after a normal breath, about 3000 millilitres.

Expiratory reserve volume: the extra air you can force out after a normal breath, about 1100 millilitres.

Residual volume: the air that always stays in the lungs, even after maximal expiration, about 1200 millilitres. It keeps the alveoli open.

Capacities are sums of volumes. Inspiratory capacity is tidal volume plus inspiratory reserve: 3500. Functional residual capacity is expiratory reserve plus residual volume: 2300. Your slide calls it "functional reserve capacity", but the correct name is functional residual capacity. Vital capacity is tidal volume plus both reserves: 4600. Total lung capacity is vital capacity plus residual volume: 5800.

Memory hook. Any capacity with "residual" in it includes the residual volume. And residual volume cannot be measured by simple spirometry, because you can never breathe it out.

Anatomical dead space is the air left in the conducting airways, which takes no part in gas exchange. That is why fast, shallow breathing is inefficient. Each small breath mostly ventilates dead space.

Control of breathing. Breathing is rhythmic and involuntary, even though the muscles can be controlled voluntarily. The respiratory centre is a group of neurons in the brainstem. It has two parts: the rhythmicity centre in the medulla, and the pneumotaxic area in the pons, which regulates the rate.

The medullary centre has two groups. The dorsal respiratory group sets the basic rhythm, firing bursts that make the diaphragm and inspiratory muscles contract. The ventral respiratory group is quiet during normal breathing, and fires when forceful breathing is needed.

Chemical control. Central chemoreceptors in the brainstem respond to rising carbon dioxide and hydrogen ions, meaning acidity, and increase the breathing rate. This is the main everyday drive to breathe. Peripheral chemoreceptors in the carotid bodies and aortic arch respond to falling oxygen, and increase rate and depth. And the inflation reflex, also called the Hering-Breuer reflex, uses stretch receptors in the airways and pleura to stop the lungs over-inflating.

Clinical link, and a classic exam scenario. Some patients with severe, long-standing chronic obstructive pulmonary disease have chronically high carbon dioxide. Their breathing depends more on the low-oxygen drive. That is why oxygen in these patients is given carefully and titrated to a target saturation, often 88 to 92 percent, with close monitoring for drowsiness.

Gas exchange happens only in the alveoli, across the respiratory membrane. It is made of the alveolar cells, the capillary cells, and their two fused basement membranes. Gases diffuse from high partial pressure to low partial pressure. Oxygen moves from the alveoli into the blood. Carbon dioxide moves from the blood into the alveoli.

Diffusion increases with more surface area, a shorter distance, greater gas solubility, and a steeper pressure gradient.

Clinical link. Every lung disease damages one of these. Emphysema destroys surface area. Pulmonary oedema and fibrosis thicken the barrier, increasing the distance.

Gas transport. Over 98 percent of oxygen is carried bound to haemoglobin as oxyhaemoglobin. Oxyhaemoglobin releases oxygen where oxygen is low. It releases even more when carbon dioxide is high, the blood is acidic, or the temperature is raised. That is exactly the condition of a hard-working muscle.

Carbon dioxide travels three ways: dissolved in plasma, bound to haemoglobin as carbaminohaemoglobin, and mostly as bicarbonate. In red blood cells, the enzyme carbonic anhydrase speeds up the reaction of carbon dioxide with water to form carbonic acid, which splits into bicarbonate and hydrogen ions. This is why the lungs help regulate blood pH. Hyperventilation lowers carbon dioxide and makes the blood more alkaline.

The respiratory system has six functions. It supplies oxygen and removes carbon dioxide. Filters inspired air. Produces sound. Contains the receptors for smell. Removes some water and heat. And helps regulate blood pH.

Pause and answer. A student inhales a peanut. Which lung is it most likely to reach, and why?

The right lung, because the right main bronchus is wider, shorter and more vertical.

Recap. Inspiration is active, driven by the diaphragm. Quiet expiration is passive recoil. Tidal volume is about 500 millilitres, and total lung capacity about 5800. Carbon dioxide is the main everyday drive to breathe. Most oxygen travels on haemoglobin, and most carbon dioxide travels as bicarbonate.

## PART 13 of 22. Respiratory assessment: history, red flags and general examination

In this part: the six key respiratory symptoms, how to explore them, the red flags that cannot wait, and what the hands, face, neck and pulse tell you before you touch the chest.

The main respiratory symptoms are dyspnoea, cough with or without sputum, haemoptysis, chest pain, wheeze and cyanosis. The first step is to establish the details of the presenting complaint.

SOCRATES, the pain tool, works for respiratory symptoms too.

Site: can the patient point to it?

Onset: when did the cough start, how long has the breathlessness lasted, and was it sudden or gradual? Sudden breathlessness makes you think of a pneumothorax or a pulmonary embolism. Gradual breathlessness makes you think of heart failure, anaemia, or chronic lung disease.

Character: is the cough continuous or occasional? What is the sputum like: its colour, amount, blood content and smell?

Sputum clinical reasoning. Clear or white: often viral or chronic bronchitis. Yellow or green: infection. Rust-coloured: classic pneumococcal pneumonia. Pink and frothy: pulmonary oedema, a medical emergency. Large amounts, especially foul-smelling and worse in the morning: bronchiectasis or lung abscess.

Radiation: does the chest pain move anywhere?

Associated symptoms: chest pain or tightness, fever or sweating, wheeze, haemoptysis, nasal congestion, ankle swelling.

Timing: has it worsened or improved over time?

Exacerbating and relieving factors: exertion, allergens, lying flat? Relieved by rest, an inhaler, or antibiotics?

Severity: can the patient speak in full sentences? How far can they walk?

Bedside tip. "Can you speak in full sentences?" is one of the fastest severity checks there is. A patient who can only speak two or three words between breaths is in severe distress, whatever the oxygen saturation says.

Then the rest of the history. Past medical history: previous operations, home oxygen, CPAP or BiPAP breathing machines, and chronic disorders such as asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, or cystic fibrosis.

Drug history: prescribed drugs, over-the-counter medicines, recreational drugs, herbal remedies, and allergies. Remember that some drugs cause cough, notably the blood pressure drugs called ACE inhibitors, and some beta-blockers can trigger bronchospasm in asthmatics.

Family history: tuberculosis, cystic fibrosis, emphysema, allergies, asthma, bronchiectasis, and clotting disorders, which raise the risk of pulmonary embolism.

Social history: smoking, alcohol, diet, occupation, travel, and sexual history.

Real-life insight. In our setting, always ask about tuberculosis contacts, because TB is spread within households. Ask about cooking with firewood or charcoal indoors, because long-term smoke exposure damages the lungs even in people who never smoked.

Cough duration. An acute cough lasts less than 3 weeks. A chronic cough lasts more than 8 weeks. The gap in between, 3 to 8 weeks, is often called subacute, common after a viral infection. As a practical rule in TB-endemic settings, a cough of two weeks or more should trigger TB screening, following your national guidelines.

Red flags. These are symptom groups needing urgent action. Your notes list: haemoptysis, hoarseness, unexplained weight loss, severe breathlessness, trouble swallowing, vomiting, peripheral oedema with weight gain, and prominent breathlessness, especially at rest or at night. Plus signs of sepsis: confusion, fever, cold peripheries, low blood pressure and fast heart rate.

For lung cancer, your notes flag smokers over 45 with a new or changed cough, or with voice changes. They also list the screening group as smokers aged 55 to 80 with a 30 pack-year history who smoke now or quit within 15 years. That was the older US criterion. The current US recommendation, updated in 2021, is ages 50 to 80 with a 20 pack-year history. Know your slide's numbers for the exam, and the updated ones for practice.

Now the general examination.

Cyanosis is a bluish colour caused by poorly oxygenated blood. There are two types. Central cyanosis is seen on the tongue and lips. It means the blood itself is poorly oxygenated. Causes in your notes: chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, type 2 respiratory failure, pulmonary fibrosis, asthma, congenital cyanotic heart disease, and pulmonary embolism.

Peripheral cyanosis is seen only in the fingers and toes, with a pink tongue. It means the blood is fine, but flow to the periphery is slow, so the tissues extract more oxygen. Common causes are cold exposure, shock, heart failure, and peripheral vascular disease. Your slide lists only central causes, so add these.

Memory hook. Look at the tongue. Blue tongue means central. Pink tongue with blue fingers means peripheral. And remember, central cyanosis always comes with peripheral cyanosis, but not the other way round.

Bedside caution. Cyanosis is a late sign, and it is hard to see in dark skin and in anaemic patients. Check the tongue, lips, gums and nail beds, and use pulse oximetry.

Oedema. Ankle and sacral oedema in a lung patient suggests right heart failure caused by lung disease, called cor pulmonale.

The face. Your notes use two classic labels. "Pink puffers" are thin, breathless, hyperventilating patients with normal colour: the emphysema picture. "Blue bloaters" are cyanosed, oedematous patients: the chronic bronchitis picture. These labels are old, and most patients have a mix, but they still appear in exams. Also look for congested neck veins and rashes.

The neck. Enlarged lymph nodes suggest tuberculosis, lymphoma, sarcoidosis, or cancer.

The skin. Rashes, such as shingles, which can cause chest pain before the rash appears. Scars from previous surgery, burns or biopsies. Pigmentation. And dilated veins across the chest, which suggest superior vena cava obstruction, most often from lung cancer.

The hands. Cyanosis and finger clubbing. Clubbing is loss of the normal angle between the nail and the nail bed, with a bulbous fingertip. Respiratory causes in your notes: lung cancer, tuberculosis, empyema, lung abscess, fibrosing alveolitis, and bronchiectasis. Many of these are chronic suppurative, meaning pus-forming, conditions.

Exam trap. Uncomplicated chronic obstructive pulmonary disease does not cause clubbing. Clubbing in a patient with that diagnosis should make you look for lung cancer.

Quick bedside test for clubbing, called Schamroth's window. Place the backs of the same finger from each hand together, nail to nail. Normally you see a small diamond-shaped gap. In clubbing, the gap disappears.

The pulse. Tachycardia above 120 per minute suggests infection or pulmonary embolism. A collapsing, large-volume, bounding pulse suggests carbon dioxide retention in type 2 respiratory failure. Carbon dioxide dilates the vessels. Look for the matching flapping tremor of the hands.

Pulsus paradoxus is an exaggerated fall in systolic pressure during inspiration, usually more than 10 millimetres of mercury. Your notes list severe asthma, status asthmaticus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, massive pulmonary embolism, and tension pneumothorax. Add cardiac tamponade, the classic cardiac cause.

Pause and answer. A patient has blue fingers but a pink tongue on a cold morning. Central or peripheral cyanosis, and is it an emergency?

Peripheral cyanosis. The blood is well oxygenated, and the cause is probably cold or poor circulation. Warm the patient and check perfusion, but a pink tongue is reassuring.

Recap. Use SOCRATES for respiratory symptoms, and check whether the patient can speak in full sentences. Cough is acute under 3 weeks and chronic over 8. Blue tongue means central cyanosis. Clubbing points to chronic pus-forming disease or cancer, not simple chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.

## PART 14 of 22. Respiratory assessment: examining the chest

In this part: the chest examination, in order. What you see, what you feel, what you hear on tapping, and the breath sounds that tell you what the lung is doing.

Inspection. Look at the rate, rhythm and effort of breathing. The respiratory deck gives a normal rate of 14 to 18 per minute. The overall adult range is 12 to 20. Look for use of the accessory muscles: the neck muscles, the shoulders, and the nostrils flaring. That means severe work of breathing, as in status asthmaticus. Look at the front-to-back diameter of the chest. Note any tenderness, from fractured ribs, metastases, or nerve pain.

Breathing patterns you can see: Cheyne-Stokes, which your notes describe as cyclical variation in depth with periods of apnoea. Hyperventilation, for example in diabetic ketoacidosis or pulmonary embolism. And hypoventilation, for example in type 2 respiratory failure.

Chest shapes. Barrel chest: an increased front-to-back diameter, so the chest looks round. It happens in advanced chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, from air trapping. Horizontal ribs are also seen in emphysema. Normally, the side-to-side width is about twice the front-to-back depth.

Pectus excavatum, or funnel chest: the sternum is sunken. Pectus carinatum, or pigeon chest: the sternum sticks out. Kyphosis and scoliosis: curvature of the spine that restricts chest expansion. Flail chest: multiple rib fractures leave a segment that moves inwards on inspiration, the opposite of the rest of the chest. That is called paradoxical movement and is an emergency. Dilated veins on the chest suggest superior vena cava obstruction, often from lung cancer.

Palpation. Feel for tenderness, masses, a pleural friction rub, and tactile fremitus. Check the position of the trachea, and chest expansion.

Tactile fremitus is the vibration you feel on the chest when the patient speaks. Place the palms, or the ulnar edges, of both hands on the chest, symmetrically. Ask the patient to say "ninety-nine". Compare side to side. If the patient does not speak English, any deep resonant word works.

The logic of fremitus is simple. Sound vibrations travel better through solid tissue than through air or fluid.

Decreased or absent fremitus means air or fluid is sitting between the lung and your hand, blocking the vibration. Think pleural effusion or pneumothorax.

Increased fremitus means the lung tissue itself is denser. Think consolidation, such as pneumonia. Your notes also list lobar collapse and tumour.

Memory hook. Fluid or air outside the lung: fremitus falls. Solid lung: fremitus rises.

The trachea. About 4 to 5 centimetres of upper trachea can be felt in the neck, between the cricoid cartilage and the sternal notch. It should be central.

A trachea pushed away from one side means something is taking up space on that side: a tension pneumothorax, or a large pleural effusion.

A trachea pulled toward one side means that side has lost volume: lung collapse, or fibrosis.

Memory hook. Push means extra stuff on that side. Pull means missing lung on that side.

Red flag. A trachea shifted away from one side, with absent breath sounds on that side, falling blood pressure, and severe distress, is a tension pneumothorax. This kills within minutes. Call for help immediately. It is treated by decompression, not by waiting for an X-ray.

Percussion. The chest percussion tones are the same five we learned. Resonant: normal lung. Hyperresonant: very loud, low-pitched and booming, over over-inflated areas such as pneumothorax, severe asthma, and emphysema. Tympanic: drum-like, over large air spaces such as a pneumothorax or the stomach. Dull: over fluid or solid, such as pneumonia, atelectasis, pleural effusion, and normally over the liver and heart. Flat: over muscle or bone.

A quick fix to your slide. The hyperresonant row lists "very loud" twice. It should read: very loud intensity, very low pitch.

Clinical reasoning. Put percussion and fremitus together and you can separate the two most common causes of a dull lung base. Dull with increased fremitus: consolidation. Dull with decreased fremitus: effusion. Same dullness, opposite fremitus.

Auscultation. Listen with the diaphragm, comparing side to side, at each level. Front and back, from the top of the lungs to the bases, in a ladder pattern, as your notes show. Ask the patient to breathe slowly and deeply through the open mouth.

There are four normal breath sounds, defined by where they are heard.

Vesicular sounds are soft and low-pitched, heard over most of the lung fields. Inspiration is longer than expiration, about two to three times longer, with no pause between them. This is what healthy peripheral lung sounds like.

Bronchovesicular sounds are heard between the scapulae at the back and in the upper central chest at the front, around the first and second intercostal spaces. They are tubular, and inspiration and expiration are about equal.

Bronchial sounds are louder, higher-pitched and hollow. They are heard over the manubrium, the upper part of the sternum. Expiration is as long as or longer than inspiration, with a short gap between them.

Tracheal sounds are the harshest, like air blown through a pipe, heard over the trachea in the neck.

Here is the key clinical point. Bronchial breath sounds heard over the peripheral lung, where you expect vesicular sounds, are abnormal. They mean consolidation. The solid lung conducts the loud central airway sounds straight to your stethoscope. Classic pneumonia.

Added sounds. Crackles, or rales. Short, popping, discontinuous sounds. They are caused by air forced through airways narrowed by mucus, pus or fluid, and by collapsed alveoli popping open. Fine crackles sound like hair rubbed between your fingers near your ear, and suggest heart failure or fibrosis. Coarse crackles are lower-pitched and longer, and suggest secretions, as in pneumonia or bronchiectasis.

Wheezes. High-pitched, musical sounds, caused by narrowed airways. They are heard in asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and bronchitis, usually loudest on expiration.

Red flag. A silent chest in a patient having a severe asthma attack is not improvement. It means so little air is moving that there is nothing left to wheeze. It is life-threatening.

Rhonchi. Lower-pitched, snoring sounds from secretions in the large airways. They are loudest on expiration and often clear after coughing. Seen in bronchitis and pneumonia.

Memory hook. Rhonchi clear with a cough, crackles do not.

Pleural friction rub. A deep, harsh, grating sound, like leather creaking, from inflamed pleural surfaces rubbing together. It is heard mainly on inspiration, and in pleuritis or pneumonia. It does not clear with coughing, and the patient often has sharp pain on breathing in.

Stridor. A continuous, high-pitched, crowing sound, mainly on inspiration, heard over the upper airway. It means partial obstruction of a large airway, the larynx, trachea or a main bronchus. It is an emergency. It is the most common added sound in children with croup, but you must always consider an inhaled foreign body.

Bedside rule for stridor in a child. Do not force the child to lie down, and do not examine the throat with a spatula. Keep them calm, upright, and with their parent, and call for senior help. Upsetting the child can turn a partial obstruction into a complete one.

Pause and answer. Right lower chest. Dull to percussion, decreased fremitus, absent breath sounds. Consolidation or effusion?

Effusion. Fluid outside the lung blocks the vibration and the sound. Consolidation would give increased fremitus and bronchial breath sounds.

Recap. The trachea is pushed away by a tension pneumothorax or large effusion, and pulled toward collapse or fibrosis. Fremitus rises in consolidation and falls in effusion or pneumothorax. Bronchial breath sounds in the periphery mean consolidation. Crackles, wheezes, rhonchi, rub, and stridor, which is an emergency.

## PART 15 of 22. Cardiovascular assessment: preparation, inspection and palpation

In this part: preparing the patient, the positions you need, the neck veins and what they reveal about fluid status, and how to find the apex beat.

The cardiovascular examination follows an organized pattern. You start by inspecting the head and neck: eyes, ears, lips, face, skull, and neck vessels. Then the upper limbs, chest, abdomen and lower limbs. Palpation covers the precordium, meaning the area of chest over the heart, and the carotid pulses. Percussion was used to find the heart borders, but imaging has largely replaced it. Auscultation covers five areas of the heart, using both diaphragm and bell.

Instruments. A stethoscope with a diaphragm and bell. A blood pressure cuff of the right size. A movable light that can shine across the skin from the side, called tangential lighting. And an examination table with a back that raises to 45 degrees.

Why tangential lighting? Because pulsations in the neck and chest cast tiny moving shadows that are invisible under light from straight above. Light from the side makes them stand out.

Preparing the patient. Explain each step. Explain that you need to expose the front of the chest, and that privacy will be provided. Explain that you will listen in several places, and that this does not mean something is wrong. That sentence matters. Patients panic when the nurse keeps listening to their heart. And explain that they will need to change position.

The positions, and why each one is used.

Fowler's position, with the head raised to 30 to 45 degrees. Used for the neck vessels and to inspect, palpate and listen over the precordium.

Left lateral position, lying on the left side. This brings the heart closer to the chest wall, making the apex beat easier to feel, and mitral sounds louder.

Sitting up and leaning forward. This brings the base of the heart forward, making aortic sounds and some murmurs easier to hear.

Sitting on the edge of the bed with legs dangling. Used to assess peripheral pulses.

Inspection. Begin with the patient upright, chest exposed. Look at the face, lips, ears and scalp. Skin colour should be even. Look for central cyanosis on the lips and tongue, and pallor that suggests anaemia.

The neck veins. The internal jugular vein connects to the right atrium with no valves in between. So the height of the blood column in that vein reflects the right atrial pressure, which is the central venous pressure. It tells you about fluid status and how well the right heart is pumping.

How to inspect. Position the patient supine, then raise the head of the bed gradually to 45 degrees. Turn the head slightly away from the side you are examining. Shine the light across the neck. Look for pulsation and distension of the jugular veins. When the patient sits fully upright, the jugular veins are not normally visible.

Clinical reasoning. Jugular veins that are distended while the patient sits at 45 degrees mean the pressure in the right side of the heart is raised. The common causes are fluid overload, right heart failure, and, in an emergency, cardiac tamponade or tension pneumothorax. Flat neck veins in a patient lying down suggest low volume, such as dehydration or bleeding.

Do not confuse the carotid artery and the jugular vein. If you see pulsation, feel the radial pulse and check whether the pulsation coincides with it. More reliable clues: the carotid pulsation is a single, firm outward thrust that you can feel. The jugular pulsation is a soft, flickering, double wave that you cannot feel with your fingers. It disappears when you press gently at the base of the neck, and it changes with position and breathing.

Memory hook. Carotid: you can feel it, and it does not care about position. Jugular: you can see it but not feel it, and it moves with position and breath.

Carotid arteries. With the patient at 45 degrees, use tangential light to inspect for pulsations. They should be visible on both sides.

The chest. Inspect the whole chest for pulsations. Observe the patient upright, then at 30 degrees, a low to mid Fowler's position. Look for pulsations, heaves or lifts over the five key landmarks, which we will name in the next part. Also look for retractions and surgical scars. A scar down the centre of the sternum suggests previous heart surgery.

A heave or lift is a sustained outward movement of the chest wall. It suggests an enlarged, overworking ventricle.

The apical impulse is usually not visible. It is located in the fifth intercostal space, at the midclavicular line, in the mitral area. It is easier to see in people with thin chest walls.

Palpation of the carotid artery. Place the pads of two or three fingers between the trachea and the sternocleidomastoid muscle. Press firmly, but not so hard that you block the artery. Normal rate is 60 to 100 beats per minute.

Red flag rule. Never palpate both carotid arteries at the same time. You can reduce blood flow to the brain, and pressure on the carotid sinus can trigger a strong vagal reflex, causing severe bradycardia and fainting. One side at a time, always. Also avoid vigorous pressure or massage over the carotid in older adults, because a plaque can break off and cause a stroke.

Percussion of the chest. It was used to outline the heart borders. Evidence shows chest X-ray and echocardiography are more accurate for detecting heart enlargement.

Palpation of the chest. Place the patient supine. Use one or two finger pads to palpate the front of the chest for pulsations, starting at the aortic area and moving down to the apex.

Locate the apical impulse precisely. Ask the patient to breathe out and hold their breath, which brings the heart closer to the chest wall. If it is hard to feel, roll the patient onto their left side and use the palm of your hand.

The apical impulse is also called the point of maximal impulse. It is normally in the fifth intercostal space at the midclavicular line. It feels like a gentle tap, and it is small, about the size of a coin. No other pulsation should be present. Note any thrills, which are palpable vibrations caused by turbulent blood flow. A thrill is a murmur you can feel.

In obese patients and those with large breasts, the apical impulse may not be palpable.

Clinical reasoning. An apex beat displaced down and to the left, out toward the axilla, suggests an enlarged left ventricle, as in heart failure or long-standing hypertension. A forceful, sustained apex suggests left ventricular hypertrophy.

Pause and answer. You feel a pulsation in the neck that is a single firm thrust, does not change when the patient sits up, and cannot be stopped by gentle pressure at the base of the neck. Carotid or jugular?

Carotid. Jugular pulsations cannot be felt, change with position, and disappear with gentle pressure.

Recap. The jugular veins reflect right atrial pressure, and they are distended in fluid overload and right heart failure. Never palpate both carotids at once. The apex beat is in the fifth intercostal space at the midclavicular line. Displaced to the left means an enlarged heart.

## PART 16 of 22. Cardiovascular assessment: heart sounds, murmurs and the peripheral circulation

In this part: where to listen, what S one and S two mean, the extra sounds S three and S four, murmurs, the pulse deficit, and the special tests for the limbs.

Auscultatory landmarks. These are the places on the chest wall where the sound of each valve is heard best. Sound travels in the direction of blood flow, so the landmarks are not over the actual valves.

Aortic area: second intercostal space, right sternal border.

Pulmonic area: second intercostal space, left sternal border.

Erb's point: third intercostal space, left sternal border, where aortic and pulmonic sounds meet.

Tricuspid area: fourth intercostal space, left sternal border.

Mitral area: fifth intercostal space, left midclavicular line, which is the apex.

Memory hook: "All Physicians Enjoy Taking Money." Aortic, Pulmonic, Erb's, Tricuspid, Mitral, moving from the upper right down to the apex. And the intercostal spaces go 2, 2, 3, 4, 5.

Technique. Place the diaphragm on the chest, start at the aortic area, and work down to the apex in a Z pattern. Assess heart rate and rhythm first.

The normal heart sounds. S one, the "lub", is the closure of the mitral and tricuspid valves. It marks the start of systole. S two, the "dub", is the closure of the aortic and pulmonic valves. It marks the start of diastole. Together they make up one cardiac cycle.

Tip for telling them apart. S one happens at the same time as the carotid pulse. Feel the carotid while you listen. The sound that lines up with the pulse is S one.

Normal findings. Rate 60 to 100, with a regular rhythm. The apical and peripheral pulses should be synchronous. In the aortic and pulmonic areas, near the base of the heart, S two is louder than S one. In the tricuspid area, S one and S two are about equal. In the mitral area, at the apex, S one is louder. Normally there are no extra sounds.

Memory hook. S two is loudest at the base, where the aortic and pulmonic valves are. S one is loudest at the apex, near the mitral valve.

Extra heart sounds. Roll the patient onto the left side and listen at the apex with the bell for S three, S four, or murmurs. Then sit the patient up, leaning forward, breathing out, and listen at the aortic and pulmonic areas. Some murmurs appear or fade with position.

S three is a low-pitched sound just after S two, during the rapid filling phase of diastole. It is physiological in children and young adults, and usually disappears between ages 25 and 35. In older adults it is pathological, and is associated with left ventricular failure. Blood rushes into a failing, overfilled ventricle, and the ventricle wall vibrates.

It sounds like "Ken-TUCK-y", with the extra beat at the end.

S four is a low-pitched sound late in diastole, just before S one. Your slide says "arterial gallop", but the correct term is atrial gallop, because it is caused by the atrium contracting. The atrium pushes blood into a stiff ventricle that resists filling. It is usually pathological. Your notes link it to pulmonary hypertension and pulmonary stenosis, which cause a right-sided S four. The most common cause in practice is a stiff left ventricle, from long-standing hypertension, left ventricular hypertrophy, aortic stenosis, or ischaemia.

It sounds like "TEN-nes-see", with the extra beat at the start.

Memory hook. S three is Kentucky and means a failing, floppy, overfilled ventricle. S four is Tennessee and means a stiff ventricle. Both are low-pitched, so use the bell.

Murmurs. A murmur is a blowing or swishing sound from turbulent blood flow. Some are caused by valve defects. Others are innocent or functional, for example in pregnancy, fever, anaemia, or in healthy children, because fast-flowing blood becomes turbulent.

Your slide says most murmurs are low-pitched and best heard with the bell. That is only partly true. Low-pitched murmurs, such as mitral stenosis, are best with the bell. High-pitched murmurs, such as aortic and mitral regurgitation, are best with the diaphragm. Use both.

Describe any murmur by six characteristics: timing, loudness, pitch, quality, location and radiation. Timing means whether it falls in systole, between S one and S two, or in diastole, after S two. Loudness is graded 1 to 6. A grade 4 or higher comes with a palpable thrill.

Your notes name some murmurs and sounds, with audio examples. Mitral stenosis: a low-pitched rumble in diastole, heard at the apex with the bell, with the patient on the left side. It is classically caused by rheumatic heart disease, which is still common in our region after untreated strep throat in childhood. Mitral regurgitation: a blowing sound through the whole of systole, at the apex, radiating to the axilla. Friction rub: a scratchy sound from inflamed pericardium rubbing on itself, in pericarditis. Heard best with the patient leaning forward. Patent ductus arteriosus: a continuous "machinery" murmur. Atrial septal defect and ventricular septal defect: holes between the chambers of the heart. A ventricular septal defect typically gives a harsh murmur through systole at the left lower sternal border.

The pulse deficit. If you find an irregular rhythm, check for a pulse deficit. Your notes describe comparing the apical pulse with the carotid pulse, listening at the apex while palpating the carotid. The standard nursing method is to compare the apical rate with the radial rate, counted by two nurses at the same time, for a full minute. The deficit is the apical rate minus the radial rate.

Why a deficit happens. In atrial fibrillation, some beats come so early that the ventricle has barely filled. The heart contracts and you hear it, but too little blood is ejected to reach the wrist. So the apex counts more beats than the radial pulse. The wider the deficit, the less effective the heart is.

Now the peripheral vasculature. Inspect the limbs for colour and discoloration, symmetry, ulcers, varicose veins, oedema, and the nails. Palpate for temperature, oedema, capillary refill, and pulses.

Clinical reasoning on ulcers. Arterial ulcers are usually on the toes or outer ankle. They are punched out, painful, with a pale base, on a cold, hairless, shiny leg with weak pulses. Venous ulcers are usually above the inner ankle. They are shallow, wetter, less painful, with oedema and brown skin staining. The location and the pulse tell you which it is, and compression treatment helps venous ulcers but can harm an ischaemic leg.

Capillary refill. Press on the nail bed for 5 seconds, release, and time how long the colour takes to return. Your notes say in a healthy patient it returns in less than 1 to 2 seconds. Most guidelines use less than 2 seconds as normal in adults. A longer refill suggests poor perfusion, as in shock, dehydration or peripheral vascular disease. Check both hands, and remember that a cold room slows refill.

Peripheral pulses. Palpate with the pads of the index and middle fingers. Assess rate, rhythm, amplitude and symmetry.

Radial: the thumb side of the wrist. Brachial: the inner side of the elbow. Femoral: the inner upper thigh, in the groin. Popliteal: behind the knee, deep, often easier with the knee slightly bent. Posterior tibial: behind the inner ankle bone. Dorsalis pedis: on top of the foot.

If a pulse is difficult to feel, use a Doppler device.

Bedside tip. Always compare left and right. A pulse missing on one side only is far more worrying than both being weak. And mark a hard-to-find foot pulse with a pen, so the next nurse checks the same spot.

Allen's test. It checks the patency of the radial and ulnar arteries. It is done before arterial blood gas sampling or an arterial line in the radial artery. The patient places their hands on their knees, palms up. Compress both radial arteries with your thumbs. Ask the patient to open and close the fist several times, then open the hand. The palm should turn pink immediately, because the ulnar artery alone is filling the hand. If the palm stays pale, the ulnar supply is poor, and you must not puncture the radial artery on that side, because if it clots, the hand has no backup.

Homans' sign. Your notes describe it for deep vein thrombosis. Place the patient supine, flex the knee slightly, and sharply dorsiflex the foot. It should not cause calf pain. Pain suggests deep vein thrombosis.

Important caution. Homans' sign is unreliable. It misses many clots and is positive in many people without clots. Forceful dorsiflexion is also discouraged in case it dislodges a clot. Know it for the exam, but in practice, suspected deep vein thrombosis is assessed by looking for one-sided calf swelling, warmth, tenderness and redness, using a risk score, and confirmed by ultrasound. Report it. Do not test it repeatedly.

Buerger's test. It assesses arterial supply to the legs. The patient lies supine. Raise the legs about 12 inches, around 30 centimetres, above the heart. Ask the patient to move the feet up and down at the ankles for 60 seconds, to drain the venous blood. The skin blanches, because only arterial blood remains. Then sit the patient up with the feet dangling, and compare the colour of both feet. Normally, pink colour returns to the toe tips within 10 seconds, and the veins on top of the foot fill within 15 seconds.

Delayed return, or feet that turn dusky red when hanging down, called dependent rubor, indicates arterial insufficiency.

Pause and answer. At the apex you count 110 beats per minute. Your colleague counts 84 at the radial pulse at the same time. What is the pulse deficit, and what rhythm is likely?

The deficit is 26. The likely rhythm is atrial fibrillation, where early beats are too weak to reach the wrist.

Recap. The landmarks, from the aortic area down to the mitral: All Physicians Enjoy Taking Money. S one is valve closure at the start of systole, S two at the start of diastole. S three is Kentucky, a failing ventricle. S four is Tennessee, a stiff ventricle. The pulse deficit is apical minus radial. Allen's test comes before radial puncture, and Homans' sign is unreliable.

## PART 17 of 22. Abdominal assessment: the map, the history, and signs outside the abdomen

The word abdomen is linked to the Latin for "to hide": whatever was eaten was hidden there. The abdomen hides a lot. In this part: how to map it, which organ sits where, why pain lies about its location, the G-I history, and the signs of gut and liver disease you can find from head to toe.

Your G-I notes cover anatomy and physiology, health history, nutritional assessment, examination techniques and normal findings, the step-by-step examination, variations from health, equipment, and diagnostic tests.

Two ways to map the abdomen. Four quadrants, made by a vertical and a horizontal line through the umbilicus. Or nine regions, made by two vertical and two horizontal lines.

The four quadrants.

Right upper quadrant: liver, gallbladder and bile ducts, the end of the stomach and first part of the duodenum, the head of the pancreas, the right kidney and adrenal gland, and part of the colon.

Left upper quadrant: most of the stomach, the spleen, the left lobe of the liver, the body and tail of the pancreas, the left kidney and adrenal gland, and part of the colon.

Right lower quadrant: the caecum, the appendix, the ascending colon, and in women, the right ovary and fallopian tube, plus the right ureter.

Left lower quadrant: the descending and sigmoid colon, and in women, the left ovary and tube, plus the left ureter.

In the middle: the aorta, the bladder, and the uterus.

The nine regions, top row first, from the patient's right to left. Right hypochondriac: liver, gallbladder, right kidney. Epigastric: stomach, liver, pancreas, duodenum, spleen edge, adrenal glands. Left hypochondriac: spleen, colon, left kidney, pancreas.

Middle row. Right lumbar: gallbladder, liver, right colon. Umbilical: the umbilicus, parts of the small intestine, and the duodenum. Left lumbar: descending colon, left kidney.

Bottom row. Right iliac: appendix and caecum. Hypogastric, also called suprapubic: bladder, sigmoid colon, and the female reproductive organs. Left iliac: descending and sigmoid colon.

Referred pain. Pain is not always felt where the diseased organ is. Pain can be felt where the organ lay during fetal development, or where its nerves enter the spinal cord. Your notes give two examples. The spleen can refer pain to the left shoulder. The kidney can refer pain to the groin.

Real-life application. Left shoulder tip pain after abdominal trauma, called Kehr's sign, can mean a ruptured spleen with blood irritating the diaphragm. Appendicitis often starts as vague pain around the umbilicus, then moves to the right iliac region as the inflammation reaches the local peritoneum. And a heart attack can present as epigastric pain. Never dismiss upper abdominal pain without thinking about the heart.

The order of the G-I assessment. History. General observation. Inspection from head to toe, then focused on the abdomen. Auscultation. Percussion. Palpation.

The history related to nutrition. Who prepares your meals? Any trouble swallowing? Past abdominal problems? Any current pain, nausea or vomiting? Bowel movements: how many per day, and are they normal, diarrhoea, constipation, bleeding, or black and tarry? Loose, pale, bulky, or hard to flush? Pale, bulky, greasy stools that float and do not flush suggest fat malabsorption, as in pancreatic or biliary disease. How often do you use anything for constipation, whether prescribed, over the counter, or herbal? What medications do you take?

A special bedside test in your notes. Ask a patient with liver disease to sign their name, or draw a circle or square, every day. As hepatic encephalopathy develops, the drawing becomes increasingly bizarre. This is a cheap, daily, bedside test of brain function.

The G-I history in more detail. Change in appetite. Usual weight and any change. Difficulty swallowing. Foods that are hard to tolerate. Nausea and vomiting. Indigestion, heartburn, also called pyrosis, or belching, also called eructation. Use of antacids, and how often. Bloating after eating. Abdominal pain, and whether it is linked to eating. Alcohol use. Medications. Bowel habits: frequency, colour, consistency, diarrhoea, constipation, flatulence, recent change, laxative use. Past G-I problems such as ulcers, hepatitis, jaundice, appendicitis, colitis and hernias. Previous abdominal surgery. And past X-rays, ultrasounds, CT scans, and colonoscopy results.

Subjective data also include exposure to infectious diseases, recent stressful life events, the last menstrual period, appetite, weight gain or loss, difficulty swallowing, and food intolerances.

Why the last menstrual period? Because in any woman of reproductive age with abdominal pain, pregnancy, including ectopic pregnancy, must be considered. An ectopic pregnancy can rupture and bleed to death.

Bedside insight on pain and food. Pain that gets better with food suggests a duodenal ulcer. Pain that gets worse with food, especially fatty food, suggests gastric ulcer or gallbladder disease.

Signs of nutritional deficiency, from your nutrition table. Face: moon face in kwashiorkor, and a wasted, monkey-like face in marasmus. Eyes: dry eyes, pale conjunctivae, and Bitot's spots from vitamin A deficiency. Mouth: angular stomatitis, cracked lips, a smooth sore tongue, and spongy, bleeding gums from vitamin C deficiency. Teeth: mottled enamel and delayed eruption. Hair: dull, sparse, brittle, with loss of colour. The flag sign, alternating light and normal bands of colour, shows periods of poor and adequate nutrition. Skin: loose and wrinkled in marasmus. Shiny and oedematous in kwashiorkor, with peeling "flaky paint" patches. Nails: spoon-shaped nails, called koilonychia, which suggest iron deficiency. Muscles: wasting, especially of the buttocks and thighs. Skeleton: deformities from lack of calcium or vitamin D. Abdomen: distended, with an enlarged fatty liver. Heart: slow rate and low blood pressure. Nerves: developmental delay and weak reflexes. Blood: pallor, small bleeding spots, and a bleeding tendency. Behaviour: lethargy and irritability.

Memory hook. Marasmus is Muscle wasting, all skin and bone. Kwashiorkor is Kwashi-swollen: oedema from protein deficiency, with a big belly.

Head-to-toe signs of G-I and liver disease, from your picture slides.

General wasting: severe chronic disease.

Palmar erythema, red palms: seen in liver cirrhosis, pregnancy, and as a hereditary finding.

Asterixis, also called liver flap: ask the patient to stretch the arms out with the wrists bent back. A flapping tremor of the hands appears. It suggests hepatic encephalopathy, and also carbon dioxide retention or kidney failure.

Leukonychia, white nails, also called Terry's nails: seen in cirrhosis and low albumin.

Parotid enlargement: seen with cirrhosis, especially alcohol-related.

Hair loss: can be linked to poor nutrition.

Yellow sclera, jaundice: liver disease or blocked bile flow. Look in natural light.

Kayser-Fleischer rings: a brown ring at the edge of the cornea from copper deposits, seen in Wilson's disease, a rare inherited disorder.

Xanthelasma: yellow fatty deposits on the eyelids, which your notes link to primary biliary cirrhosis. They are also seen with high cholesterol.

Dark pigmented spots on the lips and mouth: Peutz-Jeghers syndrome, an inherited condition with polyps in the gut.

Angular stomatitis, cracks at the corners of the mouth: riboflavin or vitamin B2 deficiency, other deficiencies, or fungal infection.

Fetor hepaticus: a sweet, musty breath smell in severe liver disease, from sulphur compounds that escape the failed liver and pass out through the lungs.

Atrophic glossitis, a smooth red tongue: iron deficiency, pernicious anaemia, vitamin B complex deficiency, or coeliac disease.

White patches on the tongue: can be caused by chewing tobacco, heavy smoking or alcohol use. White patches that cannot be scraped off should be reported.

Pause and answer. A patient with cirrhosis develops a flapping tremor of the outstretched hands, and his daily drawn circle has become irregular. What is developing?

Hepatic encephalopathy. Report it early.

Recap. Know the four quadrants and nine regions, and which organs sit in each. Pain can be referred, like the spleen to the left shoulder. Always ask a woman of reproductive age her last menstrual period. Signs of liver disease: jaundice, palmar erythema, asterixis, white nails, and fetor hepaticus.

## PART 18 of 22. Abdominal assessment: preparation, inspection and auscultation

In this part: how to prepare, the order of the abdominal exam, the five causes of a swollen belly, the skin signs worth knowing, and bowel sounds.

Preparation. Provide privacy. Good lighting and a warm room. Proper draping. Ask the patient to empty their bladder first. Introduce yourself, get consent, and explain. Wash your hands.

Why empty the bladder? A full bladder can be mistaken for a lower abdominal mass, makes palpation uncomfortable, and gives false dullness on percussion.

The order for the abdomen. Inspection, measurement, auscultation, percussion, and palpation, always last. Remember: listen before you touch.

The steps. Warm your hands. Stand on the patient's right. Position the patient supine, with the arms at the sides and the knees slightly bent, which relaxes the abdominal muscles. Ask about pain before you start. Begin superficially. Leave the painful area until last. Move systematically through the nine regions, approaching the painful area last. Use the pads of the fingers, not the tips, which hurt.

Inspection. Expose the abdomen from the xiphisternum to the pubis, keeping the rest draped. Look at symmetry and contour. Is it flat, rounded, scaphoid, meaning sunken, or distended?

Look for movement. Visible peristalsis, waves moving across the abdomen, can be seen in very thin people, but otherwise suggests bowel obstruction.

Distension. Generalized distension has five classic causes, the five Fs. Fat, meaning obesity. Faeces, meaning constipation. Fetus, meaning pregnancy. Flatus, meaning gas. Fluid, meaning ascites. Some teachers add a sixth: fatal growth, meaning a large tumour. Localized distension suggests a mass or an enlarged organ.

Scars. Every scar is a history question. It also warns you that adhesions from old surgery are a leading cause of bowel obstruction.

Purple striae, stretch marks that are purple rather than silvery: Cushing's syndrome, from excess cortisol. Silvery white striae are simply old stretch marks from pregnancy or weight change.

Spider naevi, small red spots with branching vessels like legs: cirrhosis, from excess oestrogen that the damaged liver cannot clear. More than a few above the nipple line is significant.

Café au lait spots, light brown patches: neurofibromatosis, when there are several large ones.

Cullen's sign, bruising around the umbilicus. Grey Turner's sign, bruising in the flanks. Both mean blood tracking through the tissues from the retroperitoneum. Your notes link them to acute pancreatitis. Cullen's sign is also seen in ruptured ectopic pregnancy.

Memory hook. Cullen is Central, around the umbilicus. Grey Turner is Turned to the side, in the flanks.

Distended veins radiating from the umbilicus, called caput medusae: portal hypertension. Blood blocked from passing through a scarred liver finds a way around through the veins of the abdominal wall.

Ask the patient to cough, and look for pulsations, masses and bulges. Look for inguinal and umbilical hernias. Coughing raises the pressure inside the abdomen and pushes a hernia out.

Red flag. A hernia that has become tense, tender, red and cannot be pushed back may be strangulated, with its blood supply cut off. It is a surgical emergency.

A visible pulsation in the upper abdomen can be normal in thin people. A broad, expanding pulsation suggests an abdominal aortic aneurysm. Do not press deeply on it.

Auscultation. Place the diaphragm just to the right of the umbilicus, then move in an anticlockwise direction through the quadrants. Your notes also mention auscultating the four quadrants.

Normal bowel sounds are gurgles and clicks, occurring roughly every 5 to 15 seconds, or 5 to 30 per minute, varying with meals.

Your bowel sounds table.

Hypoactive bowel sounds: less frequent than normal, perhaps one every minute or longer. Causes: paralytic ileus, peritonitis, reduced bowel movement after surgery or from opioids, and late bowel obstruction.

Hyperactive bowel sounds: loud and frequent, every few seconds. Causes: diarrhoea, early bowel obstruction, gastroenteritis, anxiety.

High-pitched, rushing or tinkling sounds, called borborygmi: the bowel is straining to push contents past a blockage. Causes: intestinal obstruction, dilated bowel loops, faecal impaction, gastroenteritis. Your notes add that they can also occur in lactose intolerance.

Absent bowel sounds: no bowel movement at all. Causes: paralytic ileus, peritonitis, late obstruction, and perforation.

Exam trap, and a bedside rule. You cannot call bowel sounds absent after a quick listen. Listen for at least 3 to 5 minutes in one area before recording them as absent.

The story of bowel obstruction, told through sound. Early, the bowel fights the blockage: loud, high-pitched, tinkling sounds. Later, the bowel tires and fails: sounds fade and disappear. So silence can be worse than noise.

Bruits. Listen over the aorta in the midline above the umbilicus, and over the renal arteries on each side. A bruit, a whooshing sound, suggests an aneurysm or a narrowed artery. A renal artery bruit in a young patient with hard-to-control hypertension suggests renal artery stenosis.

Pause and answer. After abdominal surgery, a patient's belly is distended, bowel sounds are absent after 5 minutes of listening, and he is not passing gas. What is the likely cause?

Paralytic ileus. The bowel has stopped moving after surgery.

Recap. Ask the patient to empty the bladder, and palpate last. The five Fs of distension: fat, faeces, fetus, flatus and fluid. Cullen is around the umbilicus, Grey Turner in the flanks: think pancreatitis. Caput medusae means portal hypertension. Listen 3 to 5 minutes before calling bowel sounds absent.

## PART 19 of 22. Abdominal assessment: percussion, palpation and special signs

In this part: how percussion finds air, fluid and organs, how to measure the liver and detect the spleen, how to confirm ascites, how to palpate, and the named signs of appendicitis, gallbladder disease and peritonitis.

Percussion in the abdomen. The main sounds are tympany over gas-filled bowel, which is the predominant sound, and dullness over solid organs, fluid, or stool. Resonance is heard over the lung, and hyperresonance at the base of the left lung.

If you percuss downward from the lung, over the liver, then onto the bowel, the note changes from resonant, to dull, to tympanic. You can hear the anatomy change under your fingers.

Percussion does three jobs. It identifies solid organs and gas-filled hollow organs. It estimates organ size. And it tells you whether a distended abdomen is full of trapped air or fluid, meaning ascites. Percuss and palpate the side opposite the pain first, and go clockwise.

Liver span. Use the right midclavicular line. Start over the lung, where the note is resonant, and percuss downward until it becomes dull. Mark that point: the upper border of the liver. Then start low in the abdomen, over tympany, and percuss upward until it becomes dull. Mark that point: the lower border. Measure between the two marks. Normal liver span is 6 to 12 centimetres.

Clinical reasoning. A large span suggests an enlarged liver: from hepatitis, fatty liver, congestion from heart failure, or tumour. A small span can be seen in advanced cirrhosis, where the liver shrinks. And watch for false readings. Lung disease that pushes the liver down, or gas in the colon over the lower edge, can mislead you.

The spleen: Castell's point. This is the lowest intercostal space in the left anterior axillary line. Normally it is tympanic, or resonant. Ask the patient to take a deep breath. If the note changes from tympanic to dull on inspiration, the spleen is probably enlarged, because the big spleen moves down into that space. Your notes quote 82 percent sensitivity and 83 percent specificity.

Causes of splenomegaly in your notes: trauma with haematoma, portal hypertension, blood cancers, infections such as HIV and Epstein-Barr virus, which causes glandular fever, and splenic infarct. In our setting, add malaria, one of the most common causes of an enlarged spleen, and schistosomiasis, which causes portal hypertension.

Ascites: shifting dullness. With the patient supine, percuss from the midline out to the flank until the note changes from tympanic to dull. That is where the fluid starts, because fluid sinks to the sides while gas-filled bowel floats to the top. Mark it. Then ask the patient to roll onto their side, toward you, and wait about 10 seconds for the fluid to move. Percuss again at the same spot. If the dull spot has become tympanic, the dullness has shifted. Shifting dullness is positive, meaning ascites.

A note on your slide. It describes the note changing from dull to resonant at the first stage. The principle is the same. Tympanic, or resonant, over the floating bowel. Dull over the fluid. The level moves when the patient moves.

Fluid thrill. Another test for ascites. Place the palm of your left hand on one side of the abdomen. Flick a finger against the other side. Ask the patient, or an assistant, to press the edge of a hand firmly down the midline. This stops the vibration travelling through fat in the abdominal wall. If you feel a ripple arrive in your palm, the fluid thrill is positive, meaning ascites. It is usually positive only when the ascites is large.

Now palpation. Look for tenderness, meaning discomfort and resistance on pressing. Involuntary guarding: reflex tightening of the abdominal muscles that the patient cannot control. Rebound tenderness: pain when the pressing hand is released. Rigidity: a board-like abdomen. Tenderness with rigidity suggests a perforated organ. Also feel for masses, such as an enlarged organ, faeces or a tumour, and for aortic pulsation.

Voluntary versus involuntary guarding. Voluntary guarding is the patient tensing because they are nervous or ticklish. It relaxes with distraction or breathing out. Involuntary guarding does not relax, and it means peritoneal inflammation.

Light palpation with the fingers of one hand. Deep palpation with both hands, especially in obese patients, pressing 5 to 8 centimetres, in a rolling motion.

Your notes also mention coarctation of the aorta, a narrowing of the aorta. The femoral pulse is delayed compared with the radial pulse, and the blood pressure differs between arms and legs. That is why you palpate the femoral pulses.

Liver palpation. Your notes describe three methods. First, work from low to high: start in the right lower quadrant and move up toward the costal margin, so you do not miss a very large liver. Second, the bimanual method: place your left hand behind the patient, under the 11th and 12th ribs, and lift. Place your right hand on the right upper quadrant, below the costal margin, pressing in and up. Ask the patient to take a deep breath. The liver moves down, and its edge may touch your fingers. Third, the hooking technique: stand beside the patient's chest, curl your fingers under the costal margin, and feel the edge as the patient breathes in. Normally you feel little or nothing. If you can feel the edge, it should be smooth, firm and not tender.

Spleen palpation. Place your left hand behind the left lower ribs and lift. Place your right hand on the left upper quadrant, pressing in and up, and ask for a deep breath. The spleen is normally not palpable. It must be enlarged about three times its normal size before you can feel it.

Red flag. If you feel the spleen, stop and refer. An enlarged spleen is fragile and can rupture with rough palpation. Be especially gentle in patients with glandular fever or malaria.

The special signs.

Rebound tenderness, or Blumberg's sign. Choose a site away from the painful area. Press down slowly and deeply, then quickly release. Pain on release is positive. It indicates inflammation of the peritoneum, as in peritonitis or appendicitis. Some clinicians now prefer gentler tests, such as asking the patient to cough, or percussion tenderness, which reveal the same inflammation with less pain.

McBurney's point. One-third of the way from the right anterior superior iliac spine, the bony point at the front of the hip, to the umbilicus. Tenderness there suggests appendicitis.

Rovsing's sign. Press slowly and gently in the left lower quadrant, then release. Pain in the right lower quadrant is positive, suggesting appendicitis. Pressure on one side moves gas and irritates the inflamed peritoneum on the other side.

Obturator test. The patient lies supine with the right hip and knee bent to 90 degrees. Hold the ankle with one hand and the knee with the other, and rotate the hip: knee outward, then knee inward. Pain suggests an inflamed pelvic appendix lying against the obturator muscle. It supports, but does not prove, appendicitis.

Psoas sign, listed under the peritoneal signs. Ask the patient to raise the straight right leg against your hand, or have them lie on their left side while you extend the right hip. Pain suggests an inflamed appendix lying behind the caecum, against the psoas muscle.

Aaron's sign. Pain or distress in the epigastric, umbilical or heart region during steady pressure on McBurney's point. Your notes link it to chronic appendicitis.

Murphy's sign, for the gallbladder. Press under the right costal margin and ask the patient to take a deep breath. The breath pushes the liver and gallbladder down onto your fingers. If the gallbladder is inflamed, the patient catches their breath and stops breathing in midway because of pain. That is a positive sign of acute cholecystitis.

Heel thump. Your notes list it as a peritoneal sign: pain in the abdomen when the heel is tapped, because the jolt moves the inflamed peritoneum.

The peritoneal signs, together. Rebound tenderness. Positive psoas, obturator or heel-thump tests. Rigidity, on one or both sides. Involuntary guarding. High-pitched or absent bowel sounds. Nausea, vomiting, pain and fever. All indicate peritoneal inflammation.

Bedside behaviour clue. A patient with peritonitis lies very still, because any movement hurts. A patient with colic, such as kidney stones or bowel colic, cannot keep still and writhes around.

Finally, cover the patient.

Sample documentation from your notes. "Reports good appetite. No constipation, nausea or diarrhoea. Abdomen soft, rounded and symmetrical, without distension. No lesions, scars or visible peristalsis. Aorta midline without bruit or visible pulsation. Umbilicus inverted and midline, no hernia. Bowel sounds present in all four quadrants. Liver, kidneys and spleen not palpable. No tenderness on palpation. Voiding well and denies laxative use."

Notice it describes findings. It never simply says "normal abdomen".

Diagnostics. Standard imaging includes the upper G-I series, ultrasound, MRI, CT scans and X-rays. A barium swallow or barium meal outlines the upper gut, and a barium enema outlines the colon. Biopsies can be taken. Sigmoidoscopy and upper G-I endoscopy look directly for polyps, lesions, and precancerous and cancerous changes.

An erect chest X-ray can show air under the diaphragm, which diagnoses a perforated gut. Your notes warn that it can delay treatment. In practice, a patient with a rigid, silent abdomen and shock needs a surgeon urgently, not a queue for an X-ray.

Pause and answer. Pressing the left lower quadrant causes pain in the right lower quadrant. Name the sign and the likely diagnosis.

Rovsing's sign. It suggests appendicitis.

Recap. Normal liver span is 6 to 12 centimetres. Dullness at Castell's point on inspiration suggests an enlarged spleen. Shifting dullness and fluid thrill confirm ascites. Rebound, Rovsing's, psoas and obturator signs point to appendicitis, and Murphy's sign to cholecystitis. A still, rigid patient suggests peritonitis.

## PART 20 of 22. Genitourinary system: anatomy, functions, and the signs that matter

In this part: the structures of the urinary and genital tracts, what the system does, and what each common urinary and genital symptom means.

The genitourinary tract includes the urinary organs and the genital organs. They are grouped together for three reasons. They share an embryological origin, the intermediate mesoderm. They lie close together. And they share structural pathways; in men, the urethra carries both urine and semen. These tissues face constant challenges, so they develop many problems: congenital anomalies, cancer, trauma, infection, inflammation, and iatrogenic injury, meaning harm caused by medical care, such as catheter damage.

The urinary tract. The kidneys are two fist-sized organs at the back of the abdomen, just below the ribcage. They filter waste from the blood, which leaves the body as urine. The functional unit of the kidney is the nephron. The ureters carry urine from the kidneys to the bladder. The bladder collects urine and empties when we urinate. The urethra carries urine out of the body.

Male-specific anatomy. The penis contains the urethra, and erectile tissue that fills with blood to create an erection. The scrotum is the external sac holding the testicles. The testicles produce testosterone and sperm. The epididymis collects and stores sperm until they mature. The vas deferens carries mature sperm from the epididymis for ejaculation. The seminal vesicles add a fluid rich in fructose, which nourishes sperm and improves their movement. The prostate adds fluid that helps form semen. The ejaculatory ducts collect prostate fluid and carry sperm into the urethra. And the bulbourethral glands, also called Cowper's glands, produce fluid that lubricates the urethra and neutralizes acid left by urine.

Female-specific anatomy. The vulva is the external genitalia: labia majora, labia minora, and clitoris. The vaginal opening, or introitus, lies between the urethra and the anus. The vagina is a tube lined with mucous membrane, from the cervix to the vaginal opening. The cervix sits between the vagina and uterus, lets fluids pass, and dilates in childbirth. The uterus is the hollow muscular organ where the fetus grows. The ovaries produce hormones and eggs. The fallopian tubes carry eggs from the ovaries to the uterus.

Clinical link. The female urethra is short, about 4 centimetres, and close to the anus. That is why women get urinary tract infections far more often than men. It is also why you teach wiping from front to back.

Functions of the genitourinary system. Excretion of waste products and urine. Regulation of blood volume, electrolytes, acid-base balance and blood pressure. Hormone production, including erythropoietin, which controls red blood cell production. Sperm production in the testes. And semen production.

Clinical link. Because the kidney makes erythropoietin, patients with chronic kidney disease become anaemic. Because it regulates potassium and acid, kidney failure causes dangerous high potassium and acidosis. That explains Kussmaul breathing in renal failure, which we met earlier.

Now the common signs.

Frequency: needing to urinate more often than usual, day or night. Urinating at night is nocturia. Causes: urinary tract infection, diabetes, bladder irritation, or prostate problems.

Dysuria: pain or burning on urination. Causes: urinary tract infection, sexually transmitted infections, bladder infection or inflammation.

Haematuria: blood in the urine, which may look pink, red or brown. Causes: urinary tract infection, kidney stones, kidney or bladder infection, or tumours.

The clues that go with haematuria. Flank pain suggests the ureter is blocked, by a blood clot, stone or tumour. Flank pain with haematuria can be a feature of renal cell carcinoma. Fever, dysuria, suprapubic pain and frequency suggest infection. A family history of kidney disease raises polycystic kidney disease, where cysts can rupture and bleed. And nephritic syndrome: inflammatory injury to the glomerular capillaries, with sudden kidney dysfunction, low urine output, haematuria, hypertension, and moderate proteinuria.

Red flag. Painless visible haematuria in an older adult is cancer of the bladder or kidney until proven otherwise. The lack of pain is not reassuring. Refer it.

Proteinuria: excretion of more than 150 milligrams of protein per day. It is usually silent, but persistent proteinuria suggests kidney disease. The urine may look foamy, as if mixed with soap.

Nephrotic syndrome: heavy proteinuria, more than 3.5 grams in 24 hours, with low albumin in the blood, and oedema.

Memory hook to separate them. Nephritic syndrome is about blood: haematuria, hypertension, and low urine output, from inflamed glomeruli. Nephrotic syndrome is about protein: massive protein loss, low albumin and swelling.

Lower abdominal pain: discomfort in the lower abdomen or pelvis. Causes: urinary tract infection, kidney stones, pelvic inflammatory disease, or other reproductive problems. Your notes warn that these signs can all indicate kidney damage, leading to acute kidney injury or chronic kidney disease.

Changes in urine colour or odour: darker, cloudy or strong-smelling urine. Causes: dehydration, diet, such as asparagus, infection, or liver problems. Tea-coloured urine with pale stools suggests blocked bile flow, as in obstructive jaundice.

Discomfort in the genital area: pain, itching or irritation. Causes: sexually transmitted infections, yeast infections, and skin conditions.

Unusual discharge from the urethra or vagina, differing in colour, consistency or odour. Causes: sexually transmitted infections, bacterial vaginosis, and other infections.

Incontinence: involuntary leakage of urine, from slight to total loss of control. Causes: weak pelvic floor muscles, neurological disorders, or urinary tract problems.

Urgency: a sudden, strong need to urinate immediately. Causes: urinary tract infection, overactive bladder, or bladder irritation.

Urinary retention: inability to pass urine even though the bladder is full. Causes: enlarged prostate, called benign prostatic hyperplasia, and neurogenic bladder, from nerve damage.

Red flag. Acute urinary retention is very painful and needs urgent drainage by catheter. But in an older or confused patient, it can present only as restlessness or new confusion. Check the bladder.

Fever and chills: with other symptoms, a sign of kidney or systemic infection.

Your notes also list general signs that can point to genitourinary disease: headache, fatigue, blurred vision, raised blood pressure, loss of appetite, cognitive changes, nausea and vomiting, chills, excessive thirst, and weight change. Many of these are signs of advanced kidney failure, as waste products build up in the blood. And your notes stress that genitourinary signs are often non-specific, and need investigation before a diagnosis is confirmed.

Pause and answer. A patient has oedema, frothy urine, and 5 grams of protein in 24 hours. Nephritic or nephrotic?

Nephrotic. Heavy protein loss above 3.5 grams, with oedema.

Recap. The kidney filters, regulates, and makes erythropoietin. Haematuria with flank pain suggests a stone or tumour, and painless haematuria in older adults is cancer until proven otherwise. Proteinuria is more than 150 milligrams per day. Nephritic is blood, nephrotic is protein.

## PART 21 of 22. Genitourinary assessment: history, examination and urinalysis

In this part: the genitourinary history, preparing for an intimate examination, the male and female examination, kidney and bladder percussion and palpation, and how to read a urine test.

The history of presenting illness. Ask about dysuria, frequency, nocturia, urgency, suprapubic pain, flank pain, perineal pain, testicular pain, hesitancy, reduced urine output, and fever. In older adults, also look for confusion, low blood pressure and shock.

Why confusion? Because in older adults, a urinary tract infection often presents with new confusion instead of classic urinary symptoms. And low blood pressure with shock means the infection has spread to the blood: urosepsis.

Past medical history. Previous urinary tract infections. Structural kidney disease, including kidney stones. An indwelling urinary catheter. Prostate enlargement. Pregnancy. Diabetes. And antibiotic use in the last 3 months.

Each one raises infection risk for a clear reason. Stones and prostate enlargement block flow, and stagnant urine grows bacteria. A catheter gives bacteria a direct path into the bladder. Pregnancy relaxes the ureters and slows flow. Diabetes puts sugar in the urine and weakens immunity. Recent antibiotics raise the risk of resistant organisms.

Family history. Familial and genetic conditions linked with urinary infection, such as structural kidney disease and kidney stones. Social and personal risk factors, especially sexual activity, and whether symptoms are related to intercourse.

Getting ready for the physical examination. Privacy, always. Hand washing should be a reflex. Gloves. A waste bin. Materials for specimen collection when needed. An examination table with sheets or towels.

Your notes add an important ethical line. Every physical touch must have a diagnostic purpose. For intimate examinations, best practice is to offer a chaperone, explain each step before you do it, and stop if the patient asks.

Inspection. Assess general appearance, overall health and comfort. With the patient lying down, inspect the external genitalia. In men: the penis, scrotum and pubic area. In women: the vulva and surrounding area. Look for redness, swelling, lesions or discharge. Inspect the abdomen for distension. A very large kidney may be visible as a mass, but usually only in a child or a thin adult. The ureters lie deep behind the peritoneum and cannot be seen or felt, except in massive hydronephrosis in an infant.

The bladder. A midline mass in the suprapubic area may be an enlarged bladder. Palpate gently. If gentle pressure on the mass makes the patient feel the urge to urinate, suspect a distended bladder. A full bladder can be hard to feel in an obese or tense patient. On percussion, a distended bladder is dull. A distended loop of bowel is tympanic.

Causes of a distended bladder. Obstruction: posterior urethral valves in a child, an enlarged or cancerous prostate, or a urethral stricture. Reduced bladder tone: neurogenic bladder, as after spinal cord injury, or a myogenic bladder, where the muscle has been over-stretched.

Bedside tip. Many wards now use a portable bladder scanner to measure the volume of urine in the bladder without a catheter. It confirms retention and avoids an unnecessary catheterization.

The male examination. Look at the penis for lesions. Ulcers suggest syphilis, herpes simplex, or chancroid. Bumps suggest genital warts, called condylomata acuminata, or molluscum contagiosum. A growth may be a carcinoma. Retract the foreskin. You may find balanitis, which is inflammation of the glans, warts, carcinoma, or a narrowed meatus.

Clinical note. Syphilis classically causes a painless ulcer. Herpes and chancroid cause painful ones. And always replace the foreskin after examination or catheterization. If it is left retracted, it can swell and trap the glans, a condition called paraphimosis, which is an emergency.

Inspect the meatus. You may find warts or a discharge. A discharge may be normal semen, or it may indicate gonorrhoea, trichomonas, or non-specific urethritis. Is the meatus on the tip of the glans, or on the underside? An opening on the underside is hypospadias. In hypospadias, the foreskin is usually incomplete on the underside.

Palpate the shaft of the penis. On the upper surface, you may find a plaque, as in Peyronie's disease, a thrombosed vein, or a carcinoma. On the underside, along the urethra, you may find hardening from carcinoma, stricture, or a foreign body.

The scrotum. Inspect the scrotal wall. Any skin condition can affect it: sebaceous cysts, impetigo, genital warts, cherry angiomas, lichen planus, psoriasis, and angiokeratomas. Scrotal oedema may come from systemic causes, such as liver, kidney or heart failure, or fluid overload, which is often seen during burns treatment. Enlargement may also come from disease inside the scrotum.

The testis is egg-shaped and smooth, roughly 3 by 5 centimetres, though size varies between individuals. Both testes are usually the same size in one person, unless disease is present.

The epididymis lies on the back of the testis like a crescent. It is soft, uniform, and tender when squeezed. Its head is slightly larger and lies at the top; the tail is smaller.

The spermatic cord contains blood vessels, the vas deferens, fat and nerves. The vas feels like a distinct firm cord, usually at the back of the spermatic cord.

Transillumination. In a dark room, shine a bright light through the scrotum, usually from behind. Fluid lets light through; solid tissue does not.

Clinical reasoning. A swelling that glows is fluid, such as a hydrocele. A swelling that does not glow is solid or contains blood, and a solid testicular lump must be treated as possible cancer until proven otherwise.

Red flag. Sudden, severe testicular pain, especially in a boy or young man, with a high-riding testis, is testicular torsion until proven otherwise. The testis can die within about 6 hours. It is a surgical emergency. Do not wait for tests.

The female examination. Inspect for swelling, bruising, rashes, lesions, nodules, masses or discharge.

External genital examination. Put on gloves. The examiner starts in a sitting position at the foot of the table. Tell the patient, both verbally and with a gentle touch on the thigh, that the examination is starting. Inspect and identify the external structures: mons pubis, labia majora, labia minora, clitoris, urethral meatus, introitus, perineum and anus.

Why a touch on the thigh first? Because a sudden touch on the genitals is startling. A touch on a neutral area first lets the patient prepare and relax.

The speculum examination. Check the speculum first, both mechanically and for temperature. Warm it with water and use lubricant. Tell the patient, verbally and by touch, that the speculum is about to go in.

Hold the speculum closed, at a 45-degree angle. Help the patient relax the perineal muscles. One way is to place one finger, then two, at the introitus and press gently down on the perineal body, then insert the speculum partly over your fingers. Another way is to use the closed speculum itself to press gently down. In both, direct the speculum toward the posterior vaginal wall, away from the sensitive urethra, and keep gentle pressure down toward the rectum.

Rotate the speculum as you insert it, at a slight downward angle, following the natural path of the vagina. With your other hand, move the labia out of the way to avoid pinching. At full insertion the speculum is horizontal. Open the blades slowly, about 1 to 2 inches, and locate the cervix. Lock the speculum open. Inspect the cervix. Then inspect the vaginal walls as you remove the speculum. Let the speculum close completely before it passes the introitus. Dispose of it, or place it in the container for cleaning.

Why aim away from the urethra, and down toward the rectum? The front of the vagina, near the urethra, is sensitive. The back wall and perineum stretch more comfortably.

Percussion. Bladder: gently percuss the suprapubic area. Dullness suggests a full bladder. Kidneys: use blunt, fist percussion over the costovertebral angle. That is the angle between the twelfth rib and the spine, at the back. Place one hand flat there, and strike it with the fist of the other hand. Pain, called costovertebral angle tenderness, indicates kidney problems such as pyelonephritis or kidney stones. Record any abnormality.

Palpation. Palpate the suprapubic area for tenderness or masses, which may mean a distended bladder. Palpate the kidneys gently through the flanks for tenderness. Kidney palpation is usually bimanual: one hand behind the flank lifting up, the other pressing in from the front, as the patient breathes in. Normal kidneys are rarely felt in adults.

Investigations. Urinalysis is an essential part of the renal examination. Collect a midstream specimen, meaning the middle part of the stream, so the first flush washes away bacteria from the urethra and skin.

Urine abnormalities reflect three things. High blood levels of a substance that exceed what the kidney can reabsorb: glucose, ketones, conjugated bilirubin and urobilinogen. Altered kidney function: protein, or failure to concentrate urine. And abnormal contents, such as blood.

Physical examination of urine. Colour: normally light yellow to amber. Dark urine may mean dehydration. Clarity: should be clear. Cloudiness suggests cells, bacteria or crystals. Odour: normally mild. Strong odour may suggest infection or diet.

The urine dipstick, from your table.

Specific gravity reflects urine concentration. Normal range is 1.002 to 1.035. It is high when the kidneys conserve water, as in dehydration. It stays low when the kidneys cannot concentrate urine.

pH is normally 4.5 to 8.0. In renal tubular acidosis, the urine pH never falls below 5.3, even when the blood is acidic.

Glucose: small amounts can be normal. Glycosuria may mean poorly controlled diabetes, or kidney tubules that cannot reabsorb glucose.

Ketones: the test detects acetoacetate only. Ketonuria occurs in diabetic ketoacidosis, starvation, alcohol use, and very low carbohydrate diets.

Protein: from trace to 4 plus. The more protein, the more likely significant kidney disease. Most nephrotic patients have 4 plus. Blood and protein together, both 2 plus or more, called an active urinary sediment, suggest disease within the kidney itself. The dipstick is only semi-quantitative, so confirm with a urine albumin-to-creatinine or protein-to-creatinine ratio.

Blood: 1 plus or more means non-visible haematuria. The strip cannot tell haemoglobin from myoglobin. If you suspect muscle breakdown, called rhabdomyolysis, request a specific myoglobin test.

Bilirubin is not normally present. Abnormal bilirubin or urobilinogen needs investigation for haemolysis or liver and bile duct disease.

Leucocyte esterase indicates white cells in the urine, as in infection, inflammation, stones and cancers.

Nitrite: most gram-negative bacteria convert nitrate to nitrite. A positive nitrite suggests bacteria. A negative result does not rule infection out.

Clinical reasoning. Leucocytes plus nitrites, with symptoms, strongly suggests a urinary tract infection. Send a midstream sample for culture before antibiotics start, whenever possible, so the right antibiotic can be chosen.

Microscopic examination of the urine looks for cells, casts, crystals and organisms.

Blood tests for kidney disease, from your second table. Serum urea and creatinine generally rise as kidney filtration falls, but they depend on diet and muscle mass, so they are imperfect. The estimated glomerular filtration rate, or e-G-F-R, is calculated from serum creatinine. It is usually reported as normal when it is 60 or more, and it is used to classify chronic kidney disease. Creatinine clearance is a good measure but needs a 24-hour urine collection plus a blood sample. Electrolytes: potassium rises in acute kidney injury and advanced chronic kidney disease. Bicarbonate falls, as acid is retained. Calcium falls and phosphate rises in chronic kidney disease. Urate is often raised in chronic kidney disease. Plasma and urine osmolality help investigate low sodium and unexplained high urine output. Alkaline phosphatase and parathyroid hormone rise in the bone disease of kidney failure. And antinuclear and ANCA antibodies screen for autoimmune causes such as lupus and vasculitis.

Pause and answer. A woman has dysuria and frequency. Her dipstick shows leucocytes and nitrites. What is likely, and what should you do before the first antibiotic dose?

A urinary tract infection is likely. Send a midstream urine sample for culture before the first dose.

Recap. In older adults, urinary infection can present as confusion. A distended bladder is dull, midline, and gives an urge to void on pressure. Transillumination separates fluid from solid. Testicular torsion is an emergency. Fist percussion at the costovertebral angle checks for pyelonephritis. On the dipstick, leucocytes plus nitrites suggest infection.

## PART 22 of 22. Fire round and final review

This is the fire round. Twenty rapid questions across everything you have learned. Answer out loud before you hear the answer. Do not worry about missing some. Getting it wrong now is how you get it right later.

Question 1. What are the four steps of assessment, in order?

Collect, organize, validate, document.

Question 2. "The patient says her pain is 7 out of 10." Subjective or objective?

Subjective. It is the patient's report, even though it is a number.

Question 3. What percentage of diagnoses can come from the history alone, according to your notes?

About 70 percent.

Question 4. What does SOCRATES stand for?

Site, onset, character, radiation, associated symptoms, timing, exacerbating and relieving factors, severity.

Question 5. What is the order of the abdominal examination?

Inspection, auscultation, percussion, palpation.

Question 6. Which part of the hand is best for detecting temperature?

The back of the hand.

Question 7. Which percussion note do you expect over a pneumothorax?

Hyperresonance, sometimes tympany.

Question 8. A cuff that is too narrow gives a reading that is falsely high or falsely low?

Falsely high.

Question 9. Crescendo breathing, then a pause, repeating. Which pattern?

Cheyne-Stokes.

Question 10. Deep, laboured breathing with a fruity smell. Which pattern and cause?

Kussmaul breathing, from diabetic ketoacidosis.

Question 11. Blue lips and tongue. Central or peripheral cyanosis?

Central.

Question 12. The trachea is pushed away from one side, with absent breath sounds on that side and falling blood pressure. What is it?

Tension pneumothorax. An emergency.

Question 13. Dull percussion with increased fremitus and bronchial breath sounds. What is it?

Consolidation, as in pneumonia.

Question 14. Where is the mitral area?

Fifth intercostal space, left midclavicular line. The apex.

Question 15. Which extra heart sound means a stiff ventricle?

S four. Tennessee.

Question 16. A patient's palm stays pale during Allen's test. What does it mean?

Poor ulnar artery supply. Do not puncture the radial artery on that side.

Question 17. What is the normal liver span?

6 to 12 centimetres.

Question 18. Pain in the right lower quadrant when you press the left lower quadrant. Which sign?

Rovsing's sign, suggesting appendicitis.

Question 19. A swelling in the scrotum does not transilluminate. What must you consider?

A solid mass, possibly testicular cancer, until proven otherwise.

Question 20. Leucocytes and nitrites on a urine dipstick suggest what?

A urinary tract infection.
